Start here

Buy Seeed's ESP-FLY kit first. It is a 25 g quadcopter that arrives with its own printed frame, needs only basic soldering, and flies from the free ESP-Drone app over the drone's own Wi-Fi. Your P2S takes over when a frame cracks, when you build Flix (a drone whose whole frame you print), and when you print frames and mounts for bigger drones. Holding height or position adds small sensors. GPS features such as return-to-home and waypoint missions need a printed 5-inch quad commanded from QGroundControl on an Android phone. An Android phone runs every app in this guide; an iPhone is limited to the older ESP-Drone app.

What the ESP-FLY kit includes

The kit has everything needed to fly, including a 3D-printed frame, so printing is optional for your first drone. The build is soldering the motors and battery wires, loading the firmware and trimming it.

In the box
  • XIAO ESP32-S3 board with headers and antenna
  • Pre-assembled motion-sensor and motor-driver board
  • 4 coreless motors (2 clockwise, 2 anticlockwise) and 8 props
  • 1S 250 mAh battery with a JST-PH plug
  • Printed frame, standard cover and FPV-camera cover
  • Wire landing legs, battery tie and battery wires
You add
  • Soldering iron and solder (you have these)
  • Superglue, tweezers and side cutters
  • A prop remover (you can print one)
  • A USB-C data cable to load firmware and charge
  • Your phone with the ESP-Drone app
Where your printer comes in
  • Spare frames when one cracks: no store sells them, and the free MakerWorld remix is lighter and has a P2S profile
  • Covers, guards and a prop-remover tool
  • The whole Flix frame in stage 2
  • Frames and TPU mounts for the bigger drones in stages 3 and 4

Rules for flying in Sydney

  • No paperwork for hobby flying. A drone flown for fun is a model aircraft under CASA's rules, so it needs no registration, licence or Remote ID at any weight. Flying for money changes that.
  • Keep it in your own sight, below 120 m, in daylight, at least 30 m from other people, and never over crowds, beaches or busy parks. Watching the phone's video instead of the drone breaks the sight rule.
  • Airports. Sydney, Western Sydney, Bankstown and Camden each have a 5.5 km no-fly zone, with runway corridors reaching 8.5 km. A drone of 250 g or less may fly inside below 45 m if it stays off airport land and out of the corridors. Heavier drones must stay out.
  • Sydney Harbour has been a restricted area since 9 July 2026, with no hobby permission.
  • Banned for hobby drones: Centennial Parklands, Parramatta Park, Western Sydney Parklands, Callan Park, Fernhill, the Royal Botanic Garden, the Domain and City of Sydney land. National parks need written NPWS approval at least 10 days ahead.
  • Where you can fly: indoors, private land with the owner's permission, and MAAA club fields. FPV goggles outdoors are legal only at registered MAAA fields and events, with an observer.
  • Radio: Wi-Fi and 2.4 GHz ELRS are fine. Analog 5.8 GHz video is limited to 25 mW within 5,725–5,875 MHz. Use the AU_915 setting for 900 MHz ELRS.
  • Check a CASA-verified app such as OpenSky before every flight. Sydney's drone map was redrawn in July 2026.

Fly Phone-Controlled Printed Drones Legally in Sydney

Start with Seeed's ESP-FLY kit: a 25 g ESP32 quadcopter that ships with its own 3D-printed frame and a pre-assembled flight board, needs only basic soldering, and flies from Espressif's ESP-Drone phone app over the drone's own Wi-Fi, so your first drone needs no printing at all. Bought direct from Seeed (US$59.99, A$95.10 with GST) with spare batteries, props, motors and a 1S charger from Phaser FPV at Somersby and a cable and LiPo bag from Core Electronics, it costs about A$183–193 before Seeed's and Phaser's shipping, or about A$146 if you charge packs through the drone's USB-C port. Your P2S earns its keep after that, printing the ESP-FLY spare and custom frames that no store was found selling, the whole airframe of the Android-flown Flix (about A$102 in parts from Core, Phaser and AliExpress), and the frames and mounts of bigger builds. Every feature beyond self-levelling adds a sensor: height and indoor position hold cost A$27–49 in distance and optical-flow sensors, or A$190–209 for a LiteWing drone with its matched module, while GPS position hold, return-to-home, tap-to-fly and missions need a printed 5-inch ArduPilot quad commanded from QGroundControl on Android, costing about A$495, or A$624 with a backup radio. Buy LiPos in Australia when you want them this week. Local stores ship them by road only, and Phaser, an hour north of the city, lets you collect them over the counter. US stores won't send loose LiPos abroad, but AliExpress sellers do, on tracked battery-only services that take about 2–3 weeks. Beyond your soldering station you need about A$100 of hand tools, and among your filaments only TPU 85A needs extra hardware: a A$30 0.6 mm hotend and hand feeding. CASA treats hobby drones as model aircraft that need no registration or licence, but you must keep yours in your own sight, below 120 m, in daylight and outside the 5.5 km no-fly zones around Sydney, Bankstown, Camden and Western Sydney airports, although a drone of 250 g or less may fly inside them below 45 m if it keeps clear of the runway corridors and airport land. Because Centennial Parklands, the Botanic Gardens, Parramatta Park and City of Sydney land ban hobby flying, your realistic venues are indoors, private land with the owner's permission, and MAAA club fields.

Phone control, not printing, decides which drone you build

Your P2S can print almost any frame; the link between phone and drone decides which ones you can actually fly from the phone. Three arrangements exist. On ESP32 micro quads the drone runs its own Wi-Fi access point and an app's on-screen sticks fly it, which is how Espressif describes its ESP-Drone platform: "controlled by a mobile APP or gamepad over Wi-Fi" (ESP-Drone README). On brushless FPV quads a handheld radio flies and the phone shows video, configures the flight controller and runs simulators, because phone Wi-Fi reaches only about 50 m and, as one ESP32-bridge builder put it, "WiFi was not designed for real time robust control applications" (Michal Schwarz). On a GPS quad running ArduPilot the phone becomes a ground station: QGroundControl arms, takes off, lands, returns to launch, flies to a tapped map point, orbits, follows you and runs missions (QGC Fly View), while ArduPilot says to keep "a regular transmitter/receiver connected and ready for use as a backup" (ArduPilot Joystick). Android users also report download errors for the ESP-Drone APK, which is hosted on a Chinese app site rather than Google Play (esp32.com; Instructables). A spare Android phone kept as the drone's controller removes most of this risk.

Several shortcuts fail the brief. E88-class toy drones hide their flight chip's markings and code and often use the phone only for video (Circuit Digest E88 teardown); DJI's Tello, which had the best phone apps and printable shells, was discontinued in 2024 (DroneDJ); Drona Aviation's Pluto X, which invites students to print frames, sells mainly in India (ElectronicsForU); and printed radio-controlled planes such as Planeprint's need lightweight LW-PLA, which you do not have (Planeprint). None of these was priced in Australia.

ESP-FLY arrives already printed, so the printer starts with spares

Seeed's kit contains a XIAO ESP32-S3 with headers soldered on, a 2.4 GHz antenna, the pre-assembled IMU and motor-driver module, two 24 AWG battery wires, four 615 coreless motors (two clockwise, two counter-clockwise) with leads attached, eight 30 mm tri-blade props, a 1S 3.7 V 250 mAh LiPo with a JST-PH 2-pin plug, and a 3D-printed set comprising the frame, a standard top cover and an optional FPV-camera cover, plus a sticker, a battery zip-tie and four wire landing legs (Seeed ESP-FLY repo). You supply the soldering iron and solder, tweezers and cutters, a prop remover, superglue, a USB-C data cable, a computer and the phone, and the XIAO's USB-C port both loads firmware and charges the battery (same source). Because the printed frame is in the box, printing is optional for this first drone; the build is the soldering, flashing and trimming.

The printer earns its place the first time the frame cracks. No separately sold ESP-FLY frame, motor set or flight module could be found; searches turned up only the kit itself (Think Robotics; Seeed search). The free MakerWorld remix by @Bennni claims to be 2–3 g lighter than the original 4 g frame, turns the battery holder into the landing foot, and ships a P2S-listed PLA profile at 0.08 mm layers (2.3 h) with 139 recorded prints and 4.75 stars from four raters (MakerWorld remix). The author's original is a paid Cults3D download printed in ePLA at 0.12 mm layers, 10% infill and supports in about 30 minutes (Instructables; Cults3D). Either costs cents of PLA, and the same printer makes the prop remover the kit lacks: the top MakerWorld result for a whoop prop remover, "tiny whoop tool", has 819 recorded prints (Bambu search API).

Flix is where the printer becomes essential. Its repository ships the frame as STL and STEP files plus an ESP32 holder and printed washers, to be printed at 0.2 mm layers with 100% infill, and the frame is shaped around the GY-91 sensor board (Flix README). Because you have the STEP file, you can remodel the sensor mount for Core Electronics' A$4.60 MPU-6050 board, which Flix also supports, instead of importing a GY-91 (Core MPU-6050). The repository has no licence file, so any modified frame you share sits under default copyright (Flix README). The same printer covers ESP-Drone sensor builds, whose candidate frames include Printables' Micro Quad brushed frame for 8520 motors, printed at 0.2 mm with four walls and 100% infill (Printables Micro Quad).

For bigger drones the printer saves real money only on the airframe. Robofusion's Grasshopper5 5-inch frame prints in about 101 g of PETG in 4.6 hours on a P2S-listed profile (MakerWorld Grasshopper5), under A$3 of filament at Bambu's A$26.99 PETG price (OzBargain Bambu deal), against US$48.75–64.99 (about A$70–94) for carbon 5-inch frames at RaceDayQuads (RDQ). Printed frames flex more than carbon, with a PETG ManaFly reported at about 61% lower stiffness than the carbon frame it was compared with, so their real advantages are repair cost and design freedom (MakerWorld ManaFly). Whoops are the exception: the in-stock Australian electronics for a printed-frame Air65-class whoop cost A$180.60, the same as a ready-made Air65 II at A$179.95, so print whoop frames to learn or to make crash-proof TPU spares, not to save money (Next FPV Matrix 1S; Buzz Air65 II).

Each feature level adds a sensor, and GPS adds a different drone

The features you might want form a ladder in which each rung adds a sensor, grams and firmware support. The decisive break comes at GPS: no phone-flown ESP32 firmware navigates by GPS, and ESP-FC, the most advanced ESP32 flight firmware, still lists altitude hold and GPS navigation as to-do items (ESP-FC README). The only ESP32-class printed design reported with GPS, Max Imagination's 136 g ESP-Blast, has no confirmed files or control method (Notebookcheck).

Three cautions apply to the lower rungs. Optical flow needs light and floor texture; Matek's flow module, for example, specifies more than 60 lux and 8 cm to 2 m of height (RDQ Matek 3901-L0X). The software is rough: an open ESP-Drone issue reports the position estimator failing after a flow sensor is added, and LiteWing users with the positioning module log frequent packet loss (esp-drone #102; LiteWing #18). And weight decides who can carry sensors: ESP-FLY's 3 g payload leaves no room, LiteWing carries about 25 g (LiteWing wiki). The combined Matek 3901-L0X (A$45.66, or A$33.79 on sale) and MicoAir MTF-01 (A$48.24, or A$38.59 on sale) on AliExpress are serial-port modules built for INAV and ArduPilot flight controllers, not drop-in replacements for the SPI flow sensor and I²C distance sensor that ESP-Drone expects (AliExpress Matek; AliExpress MTF-01; ESP-Drone hardware docs). ESP-Drone's full extension set also lists an HMC5883 compass, which Core does not stock; it sells QMC5883P and MMC5603 magnetometers instead, whose driver support in ESP-Drone is unverified (Core search).

The battery choice deserves care. The kit's own README recommends 150–350 mAh packs while the author's build guide allows 150–450 mAh (Seeed ESP-FLY repo; Elektor Labs); the 5.5 g mylipo pack sits inside both ranges, whereas Phaser's 15 g CNHL 450 mAh pack falls outside Seeed's range (Phaser CNHL 450). Phaser notes that the mylipo plug "is JST-PH, NOT the default tiny whoop plug", and no source confirms that third-party packs share the kit battery's polarity, so check every new pack with a multimeter before plugging it in. Core's own 1S LiPos are not drone packs: their wiring is rated to only 1 A (Core 400 mAh). The HexaCharger charges to 4.20 or 4.35 V, has a 3.85 V storage mode and PH2.0 and BT2.0 ports, and draws power from a USB-C PD supply that the listing does not say is included (Phaser HexaCharger). Set it to 4.20 V for the kit's 3.7 V pack and to 4.35 V only for high-voltage (LiHV) packs such as the 3.8 V mylipo spares. Phaser's cheaper A$20.95 BetaFPV 6-port V2 lists only "LiHV 4.35V" in its specification, so unless it has an unlisted 4.2 V mode it would overcharge the kit's standard pack (Phaser 6-port V2). The spare MMW CL-0615-19 motor matches the kit's: a 6×15 mm can, 0.8 mm shaft and 19,000 KV, or about 70,300 rpm at 3.7 V against the kit's "70,000 RPM"; cut off its Micro-JST 1.25 plug, because the kit's motors solder straight to the board (Phaser CL-0615-19).

Your four filaments cover every part once TPU 85A gets a A$30 hotend

Your P2S needs almost nothing added. It is enclosed, with a 256 mm build cube, a 300 °C hotend, a 110 °C bed, and a hardened-steel nozzle and extruder gear as standard, so it officially prints PLA, PETG, TPU and even carbon-fibre nylons out of the box; it can print PLA and PETG with the cover closed while your room is below 30 °C (Bambu Wiki P2S FAQ). The exception is TPU 85A, which Bambu supports only on 0.6 and 0.8 mm nozzles, not the 0.4 mm nozzle the P2S ships with, and which no AMS can feed (Bambu TPU 85A/90A). The cheapest genuine 0.6 mm hardened-steel H2/P2S hotend is A$30 at 3D Printer Gear, in stock; Phaser has it at A$35.95, which you can add to a drone order, and DREMC's A$28.95 Trianglelab clone has a fixed nozzle (3D Printer Gear; Phaser hotend; DREMC). Buy two if you will later print carbon-fibre filament, because leftover fibres in a nozzle make TPU harder to feed (Bambu Wiki TPU guide).

Loading TPU 85A is manual: remove the top glass, detach and secure the toolhead's PTFE tube, set the spool beside and above the printer, heat to 250 °C and feed the filament straight into the extruder, pressing Load slowly so it does not wrap the gears; unload manually too, and print at 225 °C on a 30–35 °C bed with Flow Dynamics Calibration off (Bambu Wiki TPU guide; Bambu TPU 85A/90A). Your TPU 65D depends on its maker: if it is Bambu's "TPU for AMS" it is a 68D grade that feeds through the AMS, but a third-party 64–65D filament counts as "generic TPU" and runs from an external spool (Bambu TPU for AMS; Bambu AMS HT), typically at 225–245 °C and only 20–40 mm/s (KLEMA TPU 65D). Bambu says TPU must be dried, for 16 h at 90 °C on the P2S bed under a cover or 8 h at 70 °C in a forced-air oven, and recommends 12 h at 60–70 °C for PLA and 75–85 °C for PETG (Bambu Wiki drying); its datasheets call kitchen and microwave ovens "not compatible" for drying (PLA Basic TDS). An AMS HT, A$229 at 3D Printer Gear, can dry TPU 85A but only as a sealed box, not a feeder, so the bed routine is the cheaper path (3D Printer Gear AMS HT; Bambu Wiki TPU guide).

Bambu's datasheets overturn the usual assumption that PETG is the stronger frame material. PLA is about 40% stiffer (2,580 against 1,810 MPa) and keeps 89% of its strength across layers against PETG's 68%; PETG's advantage is heat, bending under load at 62 °C rather than PLA's 54 °C (PLA Basic TDS; PETG HF TDS). A car dashboard in the sun reaches 69–71 °C within an hour, so PLA parts should not ride in a parked car on a hot day (ASU). TPU 85A absorbs impact, at 124.3 kJ/m² against PLA's 26.6, but is rubber-soft (6.8 MPa) (TPU 85A TDS), and hard TPU creeps: BASF's 64D keeps 55% of its deformation after 24 h squeezed at 70 °C, so never clamp TPU under motor or stack bolts (BASF TPU 64D TDS).

Rising NSW battery fires make LiPo discipline non-negotiable

LiPo fires are a growing NSW problem: Fire and Rescue NSW counted at least 318 lithium-ion battery fires in the state in 2024, up from 272 in 2023 and 165 in 2022 (NSW EPA). Treat even a 205 mAh 1S pack with full discipline. A standard LiPo is full at 4.20 V per cell (4.35 V for high-voltage LiHV packs such as the mylipo spares), should be stored at 3.80–3.85 V if it will sit for more than about two weeks, should be landed at 3.5–3.6 V and never drained below 3.0 V, and should be charged at 1C or less, with the balance lead connected on multi-cell packs, and never unattended; a puffed pack is permanently damaged (Oscar Liang LiPo guide). LiPo bags "slow fires but don't contain them", so if you store packs in a metal ammunition box, remove its rubber seal and drill vent holes (same source). The NSW Government adds that you should use a compatible charger and battery, charge on hard non-flammable surfaces such as concrete or tiles away from doorways and exits, in a room with a working smoke alarm, let batteries cool after use before charging, disconnect once full, and stop using any battery that swells, leaks, cracks or dents; a damaged battery should be kept at least 3 m from structures and combustible materials until disposal (NSW Government).

Never put a LiPo in any household or kerbside bin. Tape the terminals or bare leads with clear tape, keep spent packs in a ventilated glass jar, and take loose batteries under 5 kg to a free B-cycle drop-off, calling ahead if you have more than 1 kg; NSW EPA says many supermarkets, hardware stores and electronics shops host the bins (B-cycle FAQs; NSW EPA). Swollen or damaged packs need a non-airtight glass container and a call to your council, transfer station or Community Recycling Centre first; NSW has more than 100 free CRCs, open to all residents except at Leichhardt and Cumberland (NSW EPA CRCs).

Bench-test every new build in a fixed order: check for shorts with the multimeter, power up through a current limit, test each motor's direction with the props off, test the failsafe with the props off, and only then fit props, matching each to its motor's direction; ArduPilot's own failsafe guide says to remove the propellers whenever a battery is connected for testing (ArduPilot radio failsafe). On phone-flown drones the failsafe test must cover losing the app: kill the app or the phone's Wi-Fi during a low hover over a soft surface and confirm the drone cuts out or lands. Never spin motors on USB power, use a battery that can supply the motors' current to avoid brownout resets, and on Android fly in airplane mode with Wi-Fi on and VPNs off, because some phones drop the drone while mobile data is enabled (Flix troubleshooting; LiteWing flight guide). On the 6S ArduPilot quad, raise the battery failsafe: its 10.5 V default equals 3.5 V per cell only on a 3S pack and would never trigger on 6S before the pack was ruined (ArduPilot battery failsafe).

CASA and NSW rules keep phone drones in sight, in daylight and off the big parks

CASA treats your drones as model aircraft, so rules replace paperwork

A drone flown purely for sport or recreation is legally a model aircraft, and model aircraft need no registration and no operator accreditation at any weight (CASR, Part 101; CASR, Part 47). A 2026 guide's claim that recreational drones over 250 g must be registered contradicts the regulations in force (PickDrones). The paperwork returns only if you fly for money: then even a micro drone becomes a remotely piloted aircraft needing registration and accreditation (CASR, Parts 47 and 101, as above). Because casa.gov.au could not be loaded, CASA's plain-English rules come from a retailer's verbatim copy: fly no higher than 120 m (400 ft); keep at least 30 m from other people; fly one drone at a time; keep it within visual line of sight, "always being able to see the drone with your own eyes (rather than through a device, screen or goggles)"; do not fly over people or populous areas such as "beaches, parks, events, or sport ovals where there is a game in progress"; fly only by day and not through cloud or fog; keep away from emergency operations; and respect privacy (Sphere Drones reproduction). Watching the phone's camera feed instead of the drone therefore breaches the line-of-sight rule, an offence carrying 50 penalty units, A$16,500 at the A$330 unit value before its 1 July 2026 indexation (CASR, Part 101; Crimes Act 1914, s 4AA).

Sydney's airspace changed on 9 July 2026

Since 9 July 2026 the Sydney Basin has four control zones side by side: Western Sydney International (Class C), Sydney Kingsford Smith (Class C, with redefined boundaries), and Bankstown and Camden (both Class D), with Bankstown's zone extending north-west (Australian Flying). All four are controlled aerodromes, so each carries the 5.5 km no-fly zone and runway corridors in the table above; whether Bankstown's and Camden's zones lapse outside tower hours was not checked, so trust the app on the day. Western Sydney International took freight from 26 July 2026, opens to passengers on 25 October, and is intended to open for night movements on 1 November (Prime Minister; Airservices), so maps and app caches from before July understate the no-fly areas in western Sydney. Sydney Harbour sits under restricted areas R407A and R407B from the same date, and CASA's permission covers only licensed remote pilots doing aerial work, so hobbyists have no standing permission there; flying in a restricted area without permission is a strict-liability offence of 25 penalty units (CASA 28/26; explanatory statement). Helicopter landing sites carry a 0.75 NM no-fly cylinder up to 400 ft, and near them or uncontrolled aerodromes you must move away and land if a manned aircraft appears, although a sub-250 g model may keep flying outside the movement area and approach paths (Part 101 MOS). Check a CASA-verified app before every flight; Wing's OpenSky is free and builds a flight brief for recreational flyers (OpenSky; CASA verified apps). The drone status of RAAF Base Richmond, Holsworthy and hospital helipads was not established from primary sources, so rely on the app there.

Parks, councils and privacy law close most of the obvious spots

Land managers add a second layer. NSW National Parks requires written approval at least 10 days ahead to launch, land or fly in any park it manages, some parks refuse recreational drones, and drones are prohibited in parks close to controlled aerodromes such as Sydney Airport (NSW National Parks; NPWS policy). Recreational drones are prohibited on all Greater Sydney Parklands land, which covers Centennial Parklands, Parramatta Park, Western Sydney Parklands, Callan Park and Fernhill Estate (Greater Sydney Parklands), and on Botanic Gardens of Sydney land, which covers the Royal Botanic Garden, the Domain, Mount Annan and Mount Tomah (Botanic Gardens guideline). The City of Sydney "does not approve drone use for non-commercial purposes" on its land (City of Sydney drone guidelines), and Northern Beaches Council has warned that flying over populated areas such as beaches without CASA authorisation is illegal (Northern Beaches Council). Other councils regulate through park signage and plans of management, which this research did not verify park by park.

Any camera you fit brings privacy law into play. Recording activities on premises with an optical surveillance device without the owner's or occupier's consent is an offence under the NSW Surveillance Devices Act 2007 carrying up to five years' imprisonment, although that offence turns on entering the premises (NSW Communities and Justice); filming a person's private act for sexual gratification without consent is a separate crime (Sydney Criminal Lawyers). Since 10 June 2025 a federal statutory tort also lets people sue for serious invasions of privacy, explicitly including "recording the person's private activities", with no need to prove damage (Privacy Act 1988, Schedule 2), so a camera micro hovering near a neighbour's yard creates civil exposure even where the criminal offence would not apply.

Radio rules let Wi-Fi and ELRS run freely but cap analog video at 25 mW

The Low Interference Potential Devices Class Licence 2025, in force since 1 October 2025, governs every transmitter here (LIPD 2025). The ESP32 drones' Wi-Fi and ESP-NOW links fall in its 2.4 GHz digital-modulation class, allowed up to 4 W EIRP, far above any ESP32 module's output; ExpressLRS's ISM_2400 mode hops across 80 channels and so qualifies for the 4 W frequency-hopping class, but a 1 W radio into an 8 dBi patch antenna reaches about 6.3 W EIRP and breaks it (same licence; ExpressLRS FHSS source). For 900 MHz ExpressLRS choose AU_915, which hops 915.5–926.9 MHz within the 1 W band, because FCC_915 transmits down to 903.5 MHz, outside it (ExpressLRS firmware options). Analog 5.8 GHz video, including the cameras on whoops and the 5-inch build, falls under the general class: 25 mW EIRP, with the whole signal inside 5,725–5,875 MHz, so popular channels such as Raceband 1 (5,658 MHz), 2 (5,695), 7 (5,880) and 8 (5,917) are out at any power and the licence-free setting is 25 mW on in-band channels (LIPD 2025). Whether DJI, Walksnail and HDZero digital systems qualify for the 4 W digital class is unclear without ACMA guidance. A Standard or Advanced amateur licence opens 2.40–2.45 and 5.65–5.85 GHz at higher power, but only for "amateur purposes", with no financial gain and your call sign sent at least every 10 minutes (Amateur Stations Class Licence 2023); club assessors put the one-off cost at about A$48 for the ACMA certificate and A$32 for a call sign (AHARS).

Conclusion

Sydney's rules reinforce the micro-first path rather than merely tolerating it. With four controlled airports, each ringed by a 5.5 km no-fly zone and runway corridors reaching 8.5 km, plus the harbour's restricted areas, and with only models of 250 g or less allowed inside those no-fly zones at all, the 25–66 g phone-flown micros are not just the cheapest drones you can build here but the ones you may legally fly in the most places, while the 600 g GPS quad belongs at an MAAA field, the same place where outdoor goggle flying becomes lawful. The printer's economics invert in the same direction: your first drone arrives pre-printed and a printed whoop frame saves nothing, so the P2S pays for itself as a repair shop for frames no store was found selling, as the only factory for Flix's airframe, and as the source of a A$3 PETG frame where a 5-inch would otherwise need A$70–94 of carbon.

The decisions that matter most are therefore logistical and legal, not technical. Two purchases outlast their stage, the HexaCharger (stages 1 and 3) and the RadioMaster Pocket (stage 3's radio is stage 4's safety net), and both sit beside the batteries you cannot sensibly import at Phaser's counter in Somersby. The rules are also moving: Western Sydney International is due to open for night movements on 1 November 2026, MAAA's FPV approval lapses at the end of August 2027 unless renewed, and a Remote ID mandate is targeted for 2030. Treat a fresh check of a CASA-verified app as part of every pre-flight, because Sydney's drone map has already been redrawn once this year.

Tiny ESP32 Quads Turn Phones Into Transmitters

This is the first, worldwide version of the research. It has the full design catalogue and technical detail. Where it disagrees with the Sydney report above on prices or rules, the Sydney report is newer and applies to you.

The most direct way to 3D print a drone and fly it from your phone is an ESP32 micro-quadcopter that creates its own Wi-Fi network, so an app on your phone becomes the transmitter and your printer makes a frame of 4–20 g. Only two printable designs pair a phone app with solid evidence that people have built and flown them. ESP-FLY is a 25 g, 50 mm quad flown with Espressif's free ESP-Drone app and sold as a $59.99 Seeed kit whose fiddly surface-mount circuit board comes pre-assembled, while Flix is an open-source quad you hand-solder from an ESP32 dev board, a motion-sensor (IMU) breakout and four transistors (MOSFETs) onto a printed frame and fly from Android only with QGroundControl or Mavlink Joystick. Both fly about five minutes, reach roughly 50 m over Wi-Fi and only keep themselves level: holding altitude adds a barometer or distance sensor costing under $20, holding position indoors adds a $20–55 optical-flow sensor, and GPS features (outdoor position hold, return-to-home, tap-to-fly, waypoint missions) need a different machine—a printed 5-inch ArduPilot quad costing about $507 including radio, batteries and charger, or about $685 with optical flow, Remote ID and HD video to the phone. You command that quad from QGroundControl on Android, and ArduPilot's documentation says to keep a handheld radio transmitter as backup. The most popular printable designs, led by the BM Aether 4 FPV frame with 12,834 downloads and 4,291 prints, are built to be flown with a radio, so on them the phone is a video screen and setup tool rather than a joystick. Your filaments cover nearly every printed part—PLA or PETG for micro frames, PETG or stronger for larger arms, TPU 65D for mounts and skids, TPU 85A for soft bumpers and pads—although TPU 85A needs a $20.99 0.6 mm hotend and hand feeding on the P2S. Budget about $100 for tools beyond your soldering station, treat even tiny single-cell (1S) batteries with full LiPo safety discipline, and keep the drone under 100 g (UK) or 250 g (US, EU, Canada) to avoid most registration, Remote ID and training rules. An Android phone opens far more of this ecosystem than an iPhone, whose drone apps are older, fewer or missing.

Phone control, not printing, decides which drone you can build

Your P2S is not the bottleneck. It is an enclosed printer with a 256 mm build cube, a 300 °C hotend, a 110 °C bed and a hardened-steel nozzle and extruder gear as standard. That means it officially prints everything from PLA to the carbon-fibre nylons that serious frames call for (Bambu Wiki P2S FAQ). The bottleneck is the link between phone and drone.

A sweep of Bambu's MakerWorld catalogue on 5 October 2026 returned 2,974 unique drone-related designs. None came from Bambu itself, and the BM Aether 4 FPV frame led the field. Its most-downloaded frame, ProgrammaDan's Goblin, has 7,664 downloads, while its only ESP32 camera-drone listing has 35 downloads and no files yet (Printables Goblin; Printables esp32 cam drone). Two printable ESP32 quads pair phone apps with real flight evidence

ESP-FLY puts a phone-flown quad in 25 grams

ESP-FLY, created by Max Imagination and sold as a Seeed Studio kit since 2026, is the closest thing to a turnkey answer to your question. It is a 50 mm-class quad (67 × 67 × 31 mm with props) weighing 18 g without a battery and 25 g with its 250 mAh 1S LiPo. Four 615 coreless motors produce about 17 g of thrust each. The brain is a Seeed XIAO ESP32-S3 stacked on a custom four-layer IMU and motor-driver board, which carries an MPU-6050 motion sensor and four SI2300 MOSFETs. It flies about 5 minutes (5.5 in testing) and reaches roughly 50 m over Wi-Fi or 200 m over ESP-NOW, Espressif's direct radio link between ESP32 chips. It offers only Angle and Acro modes. There is no altitude hold, GPS or autonomy, and the recommended payload is just 3 g (Seeed ESP-FLY repo).

The drone creates its own Wi-Fi access point (password 12345678), and you fly it with Espressif's ESP-Drone app. The app offers on-screen sticks or phone-tilt control, plus trims and a yaw lock. On iPhone it comes from the App Store. On Android it is an APK file from the Chinese hosting site pgyer.com, so you have to allow installs from unknown apps (Instructables).

The kit costs $59.99 ($49.99 each for ten or more). It includes the XIAO, the pre-assembled IMU/motor module, motors, eight props, the battery and a printed frame set with a standard cover and an FPV-camera cover. You supply a soldering iron, superglue, a prop remover and a USB-C cable. Seeed describes assembly as "basic soldering" suitable for beginners (Seeed product page; Seeed ESP-FLY repo).

Building from scratch is cheaper on paper: $37.30 ±$10 for the XIAO ($7.49), the MPU6050 ($4.40), motors ($10.99), a LiPo ($9.99) and a $0.42 JLCPCB circuit board. But assembling that board needs an SMD stencil and a reflow hot plate, which you do not own, and the original Instructable rates the from-scratch route for intermediate or advanced makers (Elektor Labs; Instructables).

For your printer there are two frame options. The author's 4 g frame STL is a paid Cults3D download. It is printed in ePLA at 0.12 mm layers, 10% infill and supports on, with a 220 °C nozzle and 60 °C bed, and takes about 30 minutes. A free MakerWorld remix by @Bennni claims to be 2–3 g lighter and turns the battery holder into the landing foot. It ships a P2S-listed PLA profile (0.08 mm layers, 2.3 h) and has 139 recorded prints and a 4.75★ rating from four raters (Cults3D; Instructables; MakerWorld remix).

The evidence that ESP-FLY flies is broad. The main build video has 1.63 million views and the Instructable 46,489. The author reports a 2.7:1 thrust-to-weight ratio and flights out to about 50 m "before losing sight" (YouTube; Instructables). Three cautions apply. First, the remix's comments ask about longer legs for thicker batteries and about other motors, but contain no explicit flight report (MakerWorld comments). Second, battery advice conflicts: 150–450 mAh in the Instructable, 150–350 mAh in the kit FAQ. Third, the "7–25 ms" latency and "100 m+" range figures in the Elektor write-up are unmeasured claims, and they conflict with Seeed's 50 m (Elektor Labs; Seeed ESP-FLY repo).

Flix trades convenience for a fully open, hand-built design

Flix, by Oleg Kalachev, is the strongest choice if you want to print the whole airframe and wire everything yourself from off-the-shelf modules. The repository has 2,044 stars and was last updated on 30 September 2026. It ships the frame as STL and STEP files, plus an ESP32 holder and printed washers. It also includes firmware of under 2,000 lines of Arduino code, MAVLink communication over Wi-Fi or ESP-NOW, a Gazebo simulator and a Python library (Flix README).

The author is candid that Flix is "not easy to assemble and set up", with "no guarantee that it will work perfectly, or even work at all" (Flix README). The documented failure causes are wrong motor voltage, the wrong IMU model or orientation, a battery that cannot deliver about 15 A (at least 15C for a 1,000 mAh pack), loosely mounted motors ("any vibration may lead to bad attitude estimation"), and firewalls or VPNs that block QGroundControl (Flix troubleshooting; Flix assembly).

Its user gallery shows a 66 g build on a QX95 frame, an ESP32-S3-CAM build that streams video, and classroom use. The demo videos have 38,899 and 18,935 views (Flix user builds; YouTube Flix v0; YouTube Flix v1).

One legal wrinkle: the repository has no licence file. Default copyright therefore governs remixing or redistributing the frame, even though a derivative project describes Flix as MIT-licensed (Sprig-Drone). There is no official cost. Adding up cited prices for an ESP32 board (a XIAO is $7.49), an MPU6050, a coreless motor set, props, MOSFETs and one battery gives roughly $30–60. That matches CircuitDigest's $30–50 for a comparable brushed ESP32 drone (Seeed XIAO; CircuitDigest).

ESP-Drone, LiteWing and a long tail of smaller projects

ESP-Drone. Espressif's ESP-Drone firmware (2,219 stars, GPL-3.0 licence) underpins both ESP-FLY and LiteWing, and it is the most capable phone-flown firmware. It runs on ESP32, S2 and S3 chips and offers Stabilize, Height-hold and Position-hold modes. The two hold modes need extension boards: a PMW3901 optical-flow sensor plus a VL53L1X time-of-flight distance sensor, an MS5611 barometer and an HMC5883 compass (ESP-Drone README; ESP-Drone hardware docs). Espressif has offered only "limited support" since December 2022. Its reference drone uses the circuit board as the frame, although the older ESPlane-FC-V1 board "requires external drone frame assembly", which is a precedent for putting ESP-Drone electronics on a printed frame.

Printable frame candidates exist: Printables' Brushed Drone Frame for 8.5 mm motors (1,157 downloads), its Micro Quad (Whoop) Brushed frame for 8520 motors (796 downloads; 0.2 mm layers, 4 walls, 100% infill), an 18 g, 110 mm "Esp32 Drone" frame in PLA on MakerWorld, and a CC0 Thingiverse frame for ESP-WROOM-32 boards (Printables Brushed Drone Frame; Printables Micro Quad; MakerWorld Esp32 Drone; Thingiverse). None of these has been validated with ESP-Drone by its designer, and no documented build runs ESP-Drone on loose modules rather than a custom board, so on this route you would be a pioneer.

LiteWing. CircuitDigest's LiteWing is the easiest phone-flown drone that offers hold modes, at the cost of printability: its 100 × 100 mm FR4 circuit board is the frame, so your printer contributes only guards, skids and pods. The current ESP32-S3 version weighs about 45 g without a battery and carries about 25 g of payload on 720 coreless motors. It sells assembled for $49 without a battery (Tindie). An optional positioning module adds VL53L1X height hold and PMW3901 position hold, both listed as "tested and working", with an MS5611 barometer "coming soon" (LiteWing wiki). LiteWing's own app, launched in September 2025, adds a height-hold mode, battery warnings, emergency stop and gradual landing, and claims support for other drones using the Crazyflie protocol (CircuitDigest). The original 2024 design, by CircuitDigest's numbers, cost $30–50 to build and weighed about 60 g. It flew 5–7 minutes on a 1,300 mAh battery, reached 30–50 m outdoors and 15–25 m indoors, and drifted in winds above 5–8 mph (CircuitDigest).

Smaller projects. A long tail of smaller projects shows that the "ESP32 hotspot plus app" idea is easy to copy but hard to mature. PicoW Copter pairs a Raspberry Pi Pico W with a printed PLA frame and an Android UDP Joystick app for $25–30, but has been inactive since May 2024 (PicoW_Copter; PicoW docs). Near misses. Four well-known platforms fall just outside your brief. M5Stack's StampFly has altitude and position hold but flies from an ESP-NOW joystick rather than a phone (stampfly_ecosystem). Brownouts, vibration and Wi-Fi drops cause most failed flights

Builders' issue trackers show the same few failure modes, and power sag comes first: when the motors spin up, battery voltage dips and the electronics can reset. One ESP32-C3 build reset above about 20% throttle (≈700 mA) even with an added 10 µF capacitor, an MPU6050's data bus locked up at about 3.7 V and rebooted another drone, a LiteWing reboot-looped on a larger battery, and a LiteWing user found the v2.6 board's 3.3 V regulator too weak at motor start and suggested one rated for at least 800 mA (esp-drone #85; esp-drone #105; LiteWing #7; LiteWing wiki comments). The cures are a battery that can supply the current (Flix's 15 A rule), never spinning motors on USB power, and an optional boost converter (Flix troubleshooting).

Vibration from loose motors corrupts the drone's estimate of its own orientation. And without optical flow, every one of these drones drifts. LiteWing's guide says minor drift "is expected", and one user found the app's 0.6° trim step too coarse to correct it (Flix assembly; LiteWing flight guide; LiteWing #12).

The link fails too. Android phones drop the drone while mobile data is on, so fly in airplane mode with Wi-Fi enabled and VPNs off (LiteWing flight guide). Drones fitted with LiteWing's positioning module logged frequent packet loss (LiteWing #18). Flix warns that joining the drone through your home router adds delay compared with connecting to the drone's own access point (Flix usage docs).

Software and parts cause trouble as well. Users report the ESP-Drone app connecting without moving the motors (esp-drone #123). ESP-Drone requires version 5.0 of Espressif's ESP-IDF toolkit, while Flix requires version 3.3.10 of the ESP32 Arduino core, so install exactly those versions (ESP-Drone README; Flix usage docs). CircuitDigest also warns that some counterfeit MPU6050 chips fail at the 400 kHz bus speed (CircuitDigest).

Altitude hold and indoor position hold fit on a phone-flown ESP32 micro, but no phone-flown ESP32 firmware supports GPS navigation. ESP-FC, the most advanced ESP32 flight firmware, still lists both altitude hold and GPS navigation as to-do items (ESP-FC README). GPS therefore means a brushless build running ArduPilot, INAV or PX4, the established open-source autopilot firmwares.

Altitude and indoor position hold fit on an ESP32 micro, with caveats

Height hold matters a great deal to a phone pilot, because it removes the need to adjust throttle continuously on a touchscreen. There are two ways to sense height. A barometer reads air pressure; even $24 toy drones like the E88 use one for altitude hold, so the technique is mature (Circuit Digest E88 teardown). A time-of-flight sensor measures distance to the floor and suits indoor flying. LiteWing's app offers a height-hold mode backed by its distance-sensor module, ESP-Drone implements both holds with its extension boards, and Flix leaves them to its community for now (CircuitDigest; ESP-Drone README; Flix user builds).

Optical flow, which works like an upside-down optical mouse, is less forgiving. It needs light and floor texture: Matek's 3901-L0X module, for example, specifies more than 60 lux and a working height of 8 cm to 2 m (RDQ). The software support is still rough. An open ESP-Drone issue reports that its position estimator stops working once an optical-flow sensor is added, and LiteWing's packet-loss reports come from drones fitted with its positioning module (esp-drone #102; LiteWing #18).

Weight also limits which drones can take these sensors. ESP-FLY's 3 g payload leaves no room, while LiteWing's roughly 25 g payload does. In practice, LiteWing with its positioning module is today's only ready-made phone-flown ESP32 drone with both holds. A DIY ESP-Drone build on a printed 8520 frame can in principle match it for about $33 of sensors, but nobody has documented doing so yet.

GPS receivers with compasses weigh 14–32 g, several times ESP-FLY's entire 3 g payload, and no ESP32 phone firmware could use one anyway. The only ESP32-class printed design reported with GPS is Max Imagination's 2026 ESP-Blast: a 136 g quad with a PETG frame, barometer and GPS, costing about $155. Neither its files nor its control method have been confirmed (Notebookcheck). A handful of documented printable airframes cover the GPS class instead.

The phone app. QGroundControl for Android is the only mature, actively maintained, full-featured phone ground station. It works with ArduPilot and PX4 (version 5.1.5, released 30 September 2026) and connects over USB-OTG cable, Bluetooth and Wi-Fi (QGC tags). Its flight screen offers arm and takeoff, land, return-to-launch, pause, change altitude, tap-the-map "Go to location", orbit, region of interest, follow-me and mission start (QGC Fly View). With ArduPilot, follow-me fails with "Home position not set" until the GPS has fixed a home point (QGC source).

iPhone owners have to choose among smaller apps whose maintenance status is unclear: SidePilot and MAV Pilot, both listed by ArduPilot; MicoAir's MicoPilot, in TestFlight beta; and CoreWing, which supports INAV and ArduPilot (ArduPilot GCS list; MicoAir; CoreWing). The SpeedyBee app configures Betaflight and INAV but not ArduPilot (App Store).

The firmware. ArduPilot is the safer choice for a phone-centred build. INAV's own documentation calls its support for MAVLink (the telemetry protocol QGroundControl speaks) "selective". It rejects the MAVLink takeoff command, and boards with 512 KB of flash memory, including every STM32F411 and STM32F722 board, have no MAVLink at all (INAV Mavlink.md). The F411-based IMLABS design above therefore cannot talk to QGroundControl.

Flight controller and phone link. Choose these together. H743 boards with built-in Bluetooth, such as the MicoAir743 V2 ($72.99, ArduPilot pre-installed) and the Holybro Kakute H7 V2 ($52, ESP32-C3 Bluetooth), connect to the phone without extra wiring, whereas the Bluetooth on SpeedyBee F405 V4 boards is "not currently usable by ArduPilot" (Pyrodrone; Holybro; ArduPilot README). For more range, a DroneBridge ESP32 board links the flight controller to the phone's Wi-Fi for 150 m or more (about 1 km over ESP-NOW if you add a second ESP32 on the ground), and a SiK telemetry radio reaches more than 300 m out of the box and plugs into an Android phone with a USB-OTG cable (ArduPilot DroneBridge; Holybro SiK). ExpressLRS (ELRS) 3.5 and later can carry radio control and telemetry on one link and forward the telemetry to the phone over the radio's Backpack Wi-Fi, provided the ELRS hardware is ESP-based and you accept a forced 1:2 telemetry ratio (ELRS MAVLink). If phone telemetry runs over 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi, use 868/915 MHz radio control to avoid the 2.4 GHz interference one mapping-drone builder reported (Evan Briggs).

Flying from the phone alone is possible but discouraged. ArduPilot documents a ground-station-only setup: you disable the throttle failsafe, skip the radio arming check, set RC_PROTOCOLS = 0, and send a takeoff or mission-start command within seconds of arming, before the copter disarms itself (ArduPilot GCS-only). Yet ArduPilot's joystick page says that "even if flying with a joystick, you should keep a regular transmitter/receiver connected and ready for use as a backup". QGroundControl warns that its on-screen thumbsticks are "not as responsive as using an RC Transmitter" (ArduPilot Joystick; QGC virtual joystick). If the phone link drops, the ground-station failsafe triggers after 5 s by default and runs a return-to-launch (RTL), SmartRTL or landing. The copter stays in that mode even after the phone reconnects (ArduPilot GCS failsafe).

The sound pattern is to command guided and autonomous flight from the phone while keeping an ELRS transmitter in hand, to set both the phone-link failsafe and the radio failsafe to return-to-launch or land, and to assign a transmitter switch to ArduPilot's "RC override enable" function so you can cut phone input instantly (ArduPilot GCS-only).

Video reaches the phone, but with lag and an Android bias

On the micro drones, the phone's Wi-Fi is already busy carrying control commands, and the cameras that fit are weak. ESP-FLY's maintainers say an ESP32 camera is "not directly" possible with the current firmware and support a 5.8 GHz analog all-in-one camera instead, which raises the drone from 25 to 28 g (Seeed ESP-FLY repo). CircuitDigest's LiteWing tutorial adds a Wi-Fi camera module taken from a toy drone, running on its own Wi-Fi network and viewed in ordinary IP-camera apps, and notes video jitter when the motors start that a battery with a higher C rating fixes (CircuitDigest tutorial). A Flix community build streams video from an ESP32-S3-CAM board (Flix-Camera-Streaming).

Simple ESP32-CAM streams manage about 10–15 frames per second from the common OV2640 camera, and the usual advice is to put control and video on separate Wi-Fi channels (ElectronicsForU). The best open ESP32 video system, hx-esp32-cam-fpv, reaches 640×360 at 30 fps up to 1024×576, with 90–110 ms latency. It warns, though, that image quality is "comparable to smartphone cameras from around 2005" (hx-esp32-cam-fpv).

For brushless quads, three routes reach a phone. The first is analog: plug-in 5.8 GHz receivers for Android's USB port cost $13–45 and deliver 640×480 at about 100 ms latency, which one reviewer reserves for "slow ground models, spectators and bench tests" (Unmanned Tech review; brushlesswhoop). iPhones cannot use them, because iOS supports external USB video devices only on USB-C iPads (Apple Developer Forums).

The second is OpenIPC digital video, which feeds the Android app PixelPilot through a USB Wi-Fi adapter (RTL8812AU). Alternatively, in APFPV mode the drone's video transmitter becomes a Wi-Fi access point the phone joins directly, viewable in PixelPilot or a browser, at 35–70 ms latency and 50–200 m range on the phone's own Wi-Fi (PixelPilot; OpenIPC APFPV). A September 2026 bench test measured OpenIPC at 42–58 ms at 1080p 60 fps, against 14–22 ms for analog and 28–38 ms for DJI O3 (idostudio), and MakerWorld already hosts a printable 2-inch frame built around RunCam's WiFiLink2 OpenIPC camera for a quad of about 73 g (MakerWorld). The third is DJI, whose O4 air unit reaches a phone only when DJI Goggles 3 share the feed to the DJI Fly app (DJI Goggles 3 FAQ); Walksnail and HDZero video reach Android only through an HDMI input, which adds delay (Oscar Liang).

Phone transmitters for Betaflight remain bench-grade experiments

No maintained app flies a Betaflight or INAV quad from a phone. The documented routes are hobby projects: an ESP32 web joystick that feeds commands into the flight controller with about 50 m of range, a phone-browser ESP-NOW transmitter for ESP-FC that its author restricts to "ground testing" and props-off debugging, and a thesis project that turns app commands into Betaflight receiver signals (Schwarz; Ishu1519; MustafaQassmieh01).

Betaflight's own guide labels a companion device acting as the sole receiver "bench-only". It recommends an override mode instead, in which a real radio receiver keeps the arm switch and the phone only overrides the sticks. Its default failsafe drops the quad after a 1.5 s grace period (Betaflight MSP guide; Betaflight Failsafe).

The phone features in ExpressLRS (its Wi-Fi web page and Backpack) and SpeedyBee's Bluetooth are documented for configuration and telemetry, not flying (ELRS Wi-Fi; SpeedyBee app). Betaflight 2026.6 now runs experimentally on ESP32-S3 chips, but its new "Betaflight Bridge" is a configuration link for iPhones, not a control link (Betaflight 2026.6 release notes).

The workable compromise is a hybrid. A cheap ELRS radio does the flying: BetaFPV's LiteRadio 3 costs $32.99 and RadioMaster's Pocket $59.99–71.50. The phone handles video, configuration (the SpeedyBee app over Bluetooth, the Betaflight App on Android over a USB-OTG cable, and the ELRS web page) and simulator practice (BetaFPV; RadioMaster; Betaflight 2026.6 release notes). FPV educator Oscar Liang notes that touchscreen flying "doesn't effectively translate to real-world flying skills", and recommends a real radio to build muscle memory (Oscar Liang).

Toy transplants, Tello shells, blimps and planes are side roads

Toy-drone transplants. The cheapest shortcut is to move a $15–40 toy drone's electronics into a printed body, and it has the weakest evidence. A January 2026 teardown of the popular E88 toy drone found a flight chip with its markings removed, a BMP180 barometer for altitude hold, a proprietary 2.4 GHz link to its handset and a phone used only to show video, and concluded there is "no access to the flight controller, no programmable features, and no open-source code" (Circuit Digest teardown).

Some E88/E99 listings do advertise "APP control", and the WiFi UAV app (more than 2.5 million downloads, updated August 2026) offers on-screen sticks (mwm.ai). But no 2024–2026 transplant guide exists. Because the toy's flight tuning is fixed and hidden, a printed shell has to copy the donor's motor spacing, weight and balance, and a forum discussion warns that re-tuning "may not be possible if tuning parameters are not exposed" (IntoFPV).

Other shortcuts. Each has a catch. Tello, which had the best phone apps and printable shells, was discontinued in 2024 and is now available secondhand only (DroneDJ). Drona Aviation's Pluto X encourages students to print their own frames and has an app updated in 2026, but sells mainly in India at ₹14,000–20,000 (ElectronicsForU; Drona). Blimps are the gentlest phone-flown printable aircraft—Blimpduino 2.0 flies 20–30 minutes on a 500 mAh battery with an ESP8266 and a phone app—but helium lifts only about 1.1 g per litre, so every 100 g of blimp needs roughly 100 L of balloon (HackSpace review; Wikipedia). Printed planes suit the P2S, but Planeprint's newer models require lightweight LW-PLA, which you do not have, and all are flown by radio; MakerWorld's GASB ONE plane prints in PETG on a P2S profile (503 g, 21.7 h) but is radio-flown too (Planeprint; MakerWorld). General-purpose maker control apps are unsuitable for multirotors: Dabble's joystick has only 15 steps (−7 to +7), and Blynk's library is "orientated around home automation where a second or two does not matter" (Dabble; Blynk).

Your four filaments cover nearly every printed part

TPU 85A needs a 0.6 mm hotend and hand feeding on the P2S

Two of your four filaments need special handling. Bambu lists TPU 85A as incompatible with every AMS (automatic filament feeder) except the AMS HT, and even that may only serve as a sealed drying box. TPU 85A is supported only on 0.6 and 0.8 mm nozzles, not the 0.4 mm nozzle your P2S ships with, so you need a $20.99 0.6 mm hotend before you can print it (Bambu TPU 85A/90A; Shop3DUniverse).

Loading TPU 85A means removing the top glass, detaching the toolhead's PTFE feed tube and setting the spool beside and above the printer, then heating the nozzle to 250 °C and feeding the filament straight into the extruder, pressing Load slowly so it does not wrap around the gears. Unloading is also manual, and printing runs at 225 °C on a 30–35 °C bed (Bambu Wiki TPU guide).

Your TPU 65D depends on who made it. If it is a third-party filament, Bambu's documents treat it as "generic TPU", fed from an external spool rather than the AMS. If it is actually Bambu's "TPU for AMS", that grade is 68D and does feed through the AMS (Bambu TPU for AMS; AMS HT). Typical 65D filaments print at 225–245 °C, at only about 20–40 mm/s, and must be dried first (3DDevice KLEMA 65D).

Bambu says TPU must be dried: 16 h at 90 °C on the P2S bed under a cover, or 8 h at 70 °C in an oven. It recommends drying PLA for 12 h at 60–70 °C and PETG for 12 h at 75–85 °C (Bambu Wiki drying). Keep a separate hotend for TPU if you later print carbon-fibre filaments: leftover fibres in the nozzle make TPU harder to feed (Bambu Wiki TPU guide).

Datasheets favour PLA for stiffness, PETG for heat and TPU for impact

Bambu's own datasheets overturn the common assumption that PETG is the stronger frame filament. PLA is about 40% stiffer, and holds 89% of its strength across layers against PETG's 68%. PETG's advantages are heat resistance and slightly more toughness. The two TPUs belong to a different class of material entirely.

PLA's weakness is heat. It starts to bend under load at 54 °C, below the 69–71 °C that a sun-baked car dashboard reaches within an hour (ASU). Hard TPU has a different weakness: it slowly deforms under sustained pressure. After 24 h squeezed at 70 °C, BASF's 64D keeps 55% of the deformation permanently, so TPU should never be clamped under motor or electronics-stack bolts (BASF 64D TDS).

Field reports on frame materials conflict. Grasshopper5's designer says PLA will not survive near hot motors and the video transmitter, and the Sub-250 author warns that PLA softens in the sun (Robofusion; Printables). Yet the Teardrop Quad's author chose PLA-Pro for "stiffness and layer adhesion" and flew a survey mission on it, and one 5-inch designer concluded that "PETG is too brittle, Nylon is too flexible, PC and PLA work well" while noting that PLA's melting point is too low (ArduPilot Discourse; MakerWorld G4ZO). The Crab frame's designer saw clear PETG crack on hard landings, PETG-CF survive crashes from up to 10 m, and PAHT-CF survive a fall of about 100 m onto grass (MakerWorld Crab). The reports conflict because each tests a different way of failing. Stiffness and layer adhesion favour PLA, heat favours PETG, and crash energy favours fibre-filled nylon or TPU. Oscar Liang's long-standing practice, "TPU and PETG 99% of the times, TPU for flexible parts and PETG for rigid parts", is the sensible default (Oscar Liang).

Orientation, infill and inserts matter more than filament brand

Print arms lying flat, so that bending loads run along the layers. Across layers, PETG keeps only 68% of its strength, and carbon-fibre filaments only 47% (PA6-CF TDS). Use solid infill wherever loads pass through. A peer-reviewed sub-250 g PETG frame printed its arms at 0.2 mm layers and 100% infill and its base plate at 40%. It reached a safety factor of 1.35 in 5 m/s crash simulations (Drones 2025, 9, 789). Flix also prints at 100%, while ESP-FLY's tiny frame uses only 10%.

Screws and mounting. Your soldering station doubles as an insert tool. Heat brass threaded inserts 10–20 °C above the print temperature (about 225 °C for PLA, 245 °C for PETG) and press them into straight holes about 1 mm deeper than the insert. Push each one about 90% of the way in, then press it flush with a flat tool and hold until the plastic sets (CNC Kitchen). Inserts cost about €9–10 per hundred (CNC Kitchen store). Make the plastic wall around any threaded hole at least as thick as the screw diameter, choose motor screws about 2 mm longer than the arm is thick so they cannot reach the motor windings, and mount the flight controller and camera on soft TPU while bolting the motors tightly to a stiff arm (Hubs; Oscar Liang screws; Oscar Liang soft mounting; Flix assembly).

Stiffness is the hidden cost of printed frames. Betaflight's tuning notes say "stiff frames… will accept more D", meaning a stiff frame can be tuned to fly more crisply. A PETG ManaFly is reportedly about 61% less stiff than the carbon frame it was compared with (Betaflight 4.3 tuning notes; MakerWorld ManaFly). On the other hand, a ManaFly frame costs only about $1 in filament.

If you outgrow your four filaments, three additions stand out. PET-CF ($44.99) is the stiffest per gram in Bambu's range, keeps its shape under load up to 182 °C and barely absorbs moisture. PA6-CF (from $42.99) is the toughest carbon option but absorbs up to 2.35% water and must be kept dry. ASA ($29.99/kg) keeps its shape up to 92 °C and resists sunlight, which suits outdoor mounts and canopies (Bambu PET-CF; PET-CF TDS; Bambu PA6-CF; PA6-CF TDS; Bambu ASA; ASA TDS; Additive-X).

LiPo discipline: 4.20 V full, 3.85 V storage, never unattended

Oscar Liang's battery guide sets the numbers. A standard LiPo is full at 4.20 V per cell (4.35 V for high-voltage LiHV) and should be stored at 3.80–3.85 V if it will sit for more than about two weeks. Land at 3.5–3.6 V per cell and never drain below 3.0 V. Charge at 1C or less (about 0.45 A for a 450 mAh battery), always with the balance lead connected and never unattended, and retire any puffed (swollen) battery, because the damage is permanent. LiPo bags "slow fires but don't contain them", so if you store batteries in a metal ammo box, remove its rubber seal and drill vent holes (Oscar Liang LiPo guide).

Never put lithium batteries in household trash or recycling. Tape the terminals and take them to a battery drop-off or hazardous-waste point (EPA). Spare batteries may travel only in carry-on luggage, each limited to 100 Wh, or 101–160 Wh for up to two spares with airline approval (FAA PackSafe). A 6S 1,300 mAh pack is about 29 Wh.

Test every new build on the bench in a fixed order: check for short circuits with the multimeter, power it up for the first time through a current limit, test each motor's spin direction with the props off, test the failsafe with the props off (ArduPilot's own failsafe instructions say to remove the propellers if a battery is connected), and only then fit the props, checking that each one matches its motor's direction (ArduPilot radio failsafe).

For phone-flown drones, the failsafe test must cover losing the app, not just the radio. Kill the app or the phone's Wi-Fi during a low hover over a soft surface, and confirm the drone cuts out or lands. Comet Drone, for example, stops its motors after 500 ms without Wi-Fi (Comet-Drone). On a 6S ArduPilot build, raise the low-battery failsafe voltage: the 10.5 V default (BATT_LOW_VOLT) equals about 3.5 V per cell only on a 3S battery, so on 6S it would never trigger before the pack was ruined (ArduPilot battery failsafe).

Conclusion

"Flying a printed drone from my phone" turns out to describe two distinct hobbies with a gap between them. In one, a Wi-Fi micro drone is flown with the phone as its transmitter. In the other, a GPS quad flies missions with the phone as its planner. In between sit the whoop-to-5-inch FPV quads that dominate the printing sites, where the phone is only a screen. Choosing between the two hobbies matters more than any choice of frame or filament. It also changes what the printer is for. For micros, the printer makes the whole airframe in half an hour. For FPV and GPS builds it mostly makes cheap, repairable accessories and replacement frames, because a printed frame is less stiff than carbon and gains instead in repair cost and design freedom.

The ecosystem is also moving toward you. LiteWing's actively updated app is replacing ESP-Drone's 2020 one. Flix has its own circuit board and position control in development. Betaflight now runs experimentally on ESP32 chips, and INAV 10 is adding MAVLink missions (INAV releases). Expect hold modes and better links to reach sub-100 g printable designs. The purchases that carry forward through every stage are an Android phone, ESP32-S3 boards, distance and optical-flow sensors, and an ELRS radio. Filament is the cheapest part of the project and the one with the least influence on how it turns out.
