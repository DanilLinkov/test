# Fly Phone-Controlled Printed Drones Legally in Sydney

Start with Seeed's ESP-FLY kit: a 25 g ESP32 quadcopter that ships with its own 3D-printed frame and a pre-assembled flight board, needs only basic soldering, and flies from Espressif's ESP-Drone phone app over the drone's own Wi-Fi, so your first drone needs no printing at all. Bought direct from Seeed (US$59.99, A$95.10 with GST) with spare batteries, props, motors and a 1S charger from Phaser FPV at Somersby and a cable and LiPo bag from Core Electronics, it costs about **A$183–193 before Seeed's and Phaser's shipping**, or about A$146 if you charge packs through the drone's USB-C port. Your P2S earns its keep after that, printing the ESP-FLY spare and custom frames that no store was found selling, the whole airframe of the Android-flown Flix (about **A$102** in parts from Core, Phaser and AliExpress), and the frames and mounts of bigger builds. Every feature beyond self-levelling adds a sensor: height and indoor position hold cost A$27–49 in distance and optical-flow sensors, or A$190–209 for a LiteWing drone with its matched module, while GPS position hold, return-to-home, tap-to-fly and missions need a printed 5-inch ArduPilot quad commanded from QGroundControl on Android, costing about **A$495, or A$624 with a backup radio**. Buy every LiPo in Australia, because local stores ship them by road only and several overseas sellers will not send them at all; Phaser, an hour north of the city, lets you collect them over the counter. Beyond your soldering station you need about A$100 of hand tools, and among your filaments only TPU 85A needs extra hardware: a A$30 0.6 mm hotend and hand feeding. CASA treats hobby drones as model aircraft that need no registration or licence, but you must keep yours in your own sight, below 120 m, in daylight and outside the 5.5 km no-fly zones around Sydney, Bankstown, Camden and Western Sydney airports, although a drone of 250 g or less may fly inside them below 45 m if it keeps clear of the runway corridors and airport land. Because Centennial Parklands, the Botanic Gardens, Parramatta Park and City of Sydney land ban hobby flying, your realistic venues are indoors, private land with the owner's permission, and MAAA club fields.

## Phone control, not printing, decides which drone you build

Your P2S can print almost any frame; the link between phone and drone decides which ones you can actually fly from the phone. Three arrangements exist. On ESP32 micro quads the drone runs its own Wi-Fi access point and an app's on-screen sticks fly it, which is how Espressif describes its ESP-Drone platform: "controlled by a mobile APP or gamepad over Wi-Fi" ([ESP-Drone README](https://github.com/espressif/esp-drone)). On brushless FPV quads a handheld radio flies and the phone shows video, configures the flight controller and runs simulators, because phone Wi-Fi reaches only about 50 m and, as one ESP32-bridge builder put it, "WiFi was not designed for real time robust control applications" ([Michal Schwarz](https://michalschwarz.github.io/rc-controller/esp32/quadcopter/f450/asgard32/schema/2019/05/07/esp32-drone-v1.0.0.html)). On a GPS quad running ArduPilot the phone becomes a ground station: QGroundControl arms, takes off, lands, returns to launch, flies to a tapped map point, orbits, follows you and runs missions ([QGC Fly View](https://docs.qgroundcontrol.com/master/en/qgc-user-guide/fly_view/fly_view.html)), while ArduPilot says to keep "a regular transmitter/receiver connected and ready for use as a backup" ([ArduPilot Joystick](https://ardupilot.org/copter/docs/common-joystick.html)). Your phone's operating system narrows the list. Android runs everything here: the sideloaded ESP-Drone APK, LiteWing's Google Play app (updated 24 March 2026), QGroundControl 5.1.5 for Android 9 and later, the Mavlink Joystick app for Flix and plug-in 5.8 GHz video receivers ([Google Play LiteWing](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.litewing.controller); [QGC tags](https://github.com/mavlink/qgroundcontrol/tags); [Mavlink Joystick](https://github.com/goldarte/mavlink-joystick)). An iPhone gets Espressif's ESP-Drone app, last updated in July 2020 and unverified on current iOS, LiteWing's iOS listing, which returned a 404 when checked, and the Crazyflie; QGroundControl publishes no iOS build, and iPhones cannot use USB video receivers ([App Store ESP-Drone](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1522247884); [App Store LiteWing](https://apps.apple.com/in/app/litewing/id6751232172); [QGC download docs](https://docs.qgroundcontrol.com/master/en/qgc-user-guide/getting_started/download_and_install.html); [Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/776912)). Android users also report download errors for the ESP-Drone APK, which is hosted on a Chinese app site rather than Google Play ([esp32.com](https://esp32.com/viewtopic.php?p=155487); [Instructables](https://www.instructables.com/Build-the-Smallest-ESP32-Drone-You-Can-Fly-With-Yo/)). A spare Android phone kept as the drone's controller removes most of this risk.

Several shortcuts fail the brief. E88-class toy drones hide their flight chip's markings and code and often use the phone only for video ([Circuit Digest E88 teardown](https://circuitdigest.com/review/everything-you-need-to-know-about-the-e88-drone-teardown)); DJI's Tello, which had the best phone apps and printable shells, was discontinued in 2024 ([DroneDJ](https://dronedj.com/2024/01/04/dji-education-drone-shutdown-us/)); Drona Aviation's Pluto X, which invites students to print frames, sells mainly in India ([ElectronicsForU](https://electronicsforu.com/technology-trends/arduino-is-for-electronics-raspberry-pi-is-for-computing-we-aim-to-become-that-for-drones-with-pluto)); and printed radio-controlled planes such as Planeprint's need lightweight LW-PLA, which you do not have ([Planeprint](https://www.planeprint.com/print)). None of these was priced in Australia.

### ESP-FLY arrives already printed, so the printer starts with spares

Seeed's kit contains a XIAO ESP32-S3 with headers soldered on, a 2.4 GHz antenna, the pre-assembled IMU and motor-driver module, two 24 AWG battery wires, four 615 coreless motors (two clockwise, two counter-clockwise) with leads attached, eight 30 mm tri-blade props, a 1S 3.7 V 250 mAh LiPo with a JST-PH 2-pin plug, and a **3D-printed set comprising the frame, a standard top cover and an optional FPV-camera cover**, plus a sticker, a battery zip-tie and four wire landing legs ([Seeed ESP-FLY repo](https://github.com/Seeed-Projects/Co-Create_ESP-FLY)). You supply the soldering iron and solder, tweezers and cutters, a prop remover, superglue, a USB-C data cable, a computer and the phone, and the XIAO's USB-C port both loads firmware and charges the battery (same source). Because the printed frame is in the box, printing is optional for this first drone; the build is the soldering, flashing and trimming.

The printer earns its place the first time the frame cracks. No separately sold ESP-FLY frame, motor set or flight module could be found; searches turned up only the kit itself ([Think Robotics](https://thinkrobotics.com/products/esp-fly-diy-micro-drone-kit-based-on-xiao-esp32-s3); [Seeed search](https://www.seeedstudio.com/catalogsearch/result/?q=ESP-FLY)). The free MakerWorld remix by @Bennni claims to be 2–3 g lighter than the original 4 g frame, turns the battery holder into the landing foot, and ships a P2S-listed PLA profile at 0.08 mm layers (2.3 h) with 139 recorded prints and 4.75 stars from four raters ([MakerWorld remix](https://makerworld.com/en/models/1530477-esp-fly-frame-drone-housing)). The author's original is a paid Cults3D download printed in ePLA at 0.12 mm layers, 10% infill and supports in about 30 minutes ([Instructables](https://www.instructables.com/Build-the-Smallest-ESP32-Drone-You-Can-Fly-With-Yo/); [Cults3D](https://cults3d.com/en/3d-model/gadget/esp-fly-an-esp32-micro-drone-body-frame-3d-design-stl-files)). Either costs cents of PLA, and the same printer makes the prop remover the kit lacks: the top MakerWorld result for a whoop prop remover, "tiny whoop tool", has 819 recorded prints ([Bambu search API](https://api.bambulab.com/v1/search-service/select/design2?keyword=whoop%20prop%20remover&limit=6&offset=0)).

Flix is where the printer becomes essential. Its repository ships the frame as STL and STEP files plus an ESP32 holder and printed washers, to be printed at 0.2 mm layers with 100% infill, and the frame is shaped around the GY-91 sensor board ([Flix README](https://github.com/okalachev/flix)). Because you have the STEP file, you can remodel the sensor mount for Core Electronics' A$4.60 MPU-6050 board, which Flix also supports, instead of importing a GY-91 ([Core MPU-6050](https://core-electronics.com.au/mpu-6050-module-3-axis-gyroscope-acce-lerometer.html)). The repository has no licence file, so any modified frame you share sits under default copyright ([Flix README](https://github.com/okalachev/flix)). The same printer covers ESP-Drone sensor builds, whose candidate frames include Printables' Micro Quad brushed frame for 8520 motors, printed at 0.2 mm with four walls and 100% infill ([Printables Micro Quad](https://www.printables.com/model/779536-micro-quad-whoop-brushed-drone-frame)).

For bigger drones the printer saves real money only on the airframe. Robofusion's Grasshopper5 5-inch frame prints in about 101 g of PETG in 4.6 hours on a P2S-listed profile ([MakerWorld Grasshopper5](https://makerworld.com/en/models/729739-grasshopper5-5-inch-long-range-fpv-frame)), under A$3 of filament at Bambu's A$26.99 PETG price ([OzBargain Bambu deal](https://www.ozbargain.com.au/node/975119)), against US$48.75–64.99 (about A$70–94) for carbon 5-inch frames at RaceDayQuads ([RDQ](https://www.racedayquads.com/products/vroom-comet-pro-5-racing-frame-kit-choose-colors)). Printed frames flex more than carbon, with a PETG ManaFly reported at about 61% lower stiffness than the carbon frame it was compared with, so their real advantages are repair cost and design freedom ([MakerWorld ManaFly](https://makerworld.com/en/models/2000546-beta-manafly-3-generative-fpv-drone-frame)). Whoops are the exception: the in-stock Australian electronics for a printed-frame Air65-class whoop cost A$180.60, the same as a ready-made Air65 II at A$179.95, so print whoop frames to learn or to make crash-proof TPU spares, not to save money ([Next FPV Matrix 1S](https://www.nextfpv.com.au/products/betafpv-matrix-1s-brushless-flight-controller-5in1-ii); [Buzz Air65 II](https://buzzfpv.com.au/products/air65-ii-brushless-whoop-quadcopter-racing)).

### Each feature level adds a sensor, and GPS adds a different drone

The features you might want form a ladder in which each rung adds a sensor, grams and firmware support. The decisive break comes at GPS: no phone-flown ESP32 firmware navigates by GPS, and ESP-FC, the most advanced ESP32 flight firmware, still lists altitude hold and GPS navigation as to-do items ([ESP-FC README](https://github.com/rtlopez/esp-fc)). The only ESP32-class printed design reported with GPS, Max Imagination's 136 g ESP-Blast, has no confirmed files or control method ([Notebookcheck](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Tiny-open-source-3D-printed-drone-hits-67-mph-and-weighs-136-grams.1249623.0.html)).

|---|---|---|---|
| Self-levelling (Angle/Stabilize) | The drone stays level; you manage height and drift | IMU already on every board | ESP-FLY, Flix, ESP-Drone, LiteWing, Crazyflie |

Three cautions apply to the lower rungs. Optical flow needs light and floor texture; Matek's flow module, for example, specifies more than 60 lux and 8 cm to 2 m of height ([RDQ Matek 3901-L0X](https://www.racedayquads.com/products/matek-3901-l0x-optical-flow-lidar-sensor)). The software is rough: an open ESP-Drone issue reports the position estimator failing after a flow sensor is added, and LiteWing users with the positioning module log frequent packet loss ([esp-drone #102](https://github.com/espressif/esp-drone/issues/102); [LiteWing #18](https://github.com/jobitjoseph/LiteWing/issues/18)). And weight decides who can carry sensors: ESP-FLY's 3 g payload leaves no room, LiteWing carries about 25 g ([LiteWing wiki](https://circuitdigest.com/wiki/litewing/)). The combined Matek 3901-L0X (A$45.66, or A$33.79 on sale) and MicoAir MTF-01 (A$48.24, or A$38.59 on sale) on AliExpress are serial-port modules built for INAV and ArduPilot flight controllers, not drop-in replacements for the SPI flow sensor and I²C distance sensor that ESP-Drone expects ([AliExpress Matek](https://www.aliexpress.com/item/1005010391563163.html); [AliExpress MTF-01](https://www.aliexpress.com/item/1005009576180246.html); [ESP-Drone hardware docs](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/espressif-esp-drone/en/latest/hardware.html)). ESP-Drone's full extension set also lists an HMC5883 compass, which Core does not stock; it sells QMC5883P and MMC5603 magnetometers instead, whose driver support in ESP-Drone is unverified ([Core search](https://core-electronics.com.au/search/qmc5883.md)).

The battery choice deserves care. The kit's own README recommends 150–350 mAh packs while the author's build guide allows 150–450 mAh ([Seeed ESP-FLY repo](https://github.com/Seeed-Projects/Co-Create_ESP-FLY); [Elektor Labs](https://www.elektormagazine.com/labs/esp-fly-the-smallest-esp32-drone-you-can-build)); the 5.5 g mylipo pack sits inside both ranges, whereas Phaser's 15 g CNHL 450 mAh pack falls outside Seeed's range ([Phaser CNHL 450](https://phaserfpv.com.au/products/cnhl-450mah-1s-lipo-battery)). Phaser notes that the mylipo plug "is JST-PH, NOT the default tiny whoop plug", and no source confirms that third-party packs share the kit battery's polarity, so check every new pack with a multimeter before plugging it in. Core's own 1S LiPos are not drone packs: their wiring is rated to only 1 A ([Core 400 mAh](https://core-electronics.com.au/polymer-lithium-ion-battery-400mah-38456.html)). The HexaCharger charges to 4.20 or 4.35 V, has a 3.85 V storage mode and PH2.0 and BT2.0 ports, and draws power from a USB-C PD supply that the listing does not say is included ([Phaser HexaCharger](https://phaserfpv.com.au/products/betafpv-hexacharger-1s-charger)). Set it to 4.20 V for the kit's 3.7 V pack and to 4.35 V only for high-voltage (LiHV) packs such as the 3.8 V mylipo spares. Phaser's cheaper A$20.95 BetaFPV 6-port V2 lists only "LiHV 4.35V" in its specification, so unless it has an unlisted 4.2 V mode it would overcharge the kit's standard pack ([Phaser 6-port V2](https://phaserfpv.com.au/products/betafpv-6-ports-1s-battery-charger-adapter)). The Phaser motor is the right one: an 8.5×20 mm can rated 1.5–3.7 V with a 1 mm shaft and 2.75 A maximum load, so four draw about 11 A, within the roughly 15 A Flix asks its battery to supply; avoid the CL-0820-15-9T pinion version ([Phaser CL-0820-15](https://phaserfpv.com.au/products/micro-motor-warehouse-cl-0820-15-coreless-brushed-motor); [Phaser 9T](https://phaserfpv.com.au/products/micro-motor-warehouse-cl-0820-15-9t-coreless-brushed-motor); [Flix troubleshooting](https://github.com/okalachev/flix/blob/master/docs/troubleshooting.md)). The battery is the compromise. No Australian store was found stocking a 1S drone pack of 1,000 mAh or more: Phaser's range tops out at the 850 mAh GNB, so expect shorter flights and fit an A30 lead instead of MX2.0, and Hobbyco's 1S hits were out of stock ([Phaser GNB 850](https://phaserfpv.com.au/products/gaoneng-gnb-lihv-1s-38v-850mah-60c-a30-cabled-lipo-battery-long-range); [Hobbyco](https://www.hobbyco.com.au/search/suggest.json?q=1s%201000mah&resources%5Btype%5D=product)). The HexaCharger has only PH2.0 and BT2.0 ports, so A30 packs need an adapter lead or an A30-capable charger such as the ViFly WhoopStor V3 at A$54.99 ([Phaser WhoopStor V3](https://phaserfpv.com.au/products/vifly-whoopstor-1s-lipo-charger-v3)). The MOSFET warning is material: LCSC (with its sister company JLCPCB) is the only source found that is certain to supply genuine UMW parts, its small-order shipping to Australia was not obtained, and Flix warns that other manufacturers' parts "might not work" ([Flix README](https://github.com/okalachev/flix)). Phone flying is Android-only: join the drone's `flix` network (password `flixwifi`) and fly with Mavlink Joystick, or with QGroundControl's virtual joystick with Auto-Center Throttle off and the tilt limit `CTL_ATT_MAX` reduced ([Flix usage docs](https://github.com/okalachev/flix/blob/master/docs/usage.md)). Its STAB mode "doesn't stabilize its position, so slight drift is possible", and altitude hold so far exists only in student builds from RoboCamp 2026 ([Flix user builds](https://github.com/okalachev/flix/blob/master/docs/user.md)).

If you want holds without pioneering, **LiteWing** is the only ready-made phone-flown ESP32 drone with both. Its seller, Semicon Lab in Jaipur, lists the drone at US$49 ("Only 9 left") and the positioning module (PMW3901 flow, VL53L1X range, about 8 g) at US$38 on Tindie, with tracked India Post at US$30 for the first item and US$15 for each extra ([Tindie LiteWing](https://www.tindie.com/products/semicon_lab/litewing-esp32-based-programmable-drone/); [Tindie module](https://www.tindie.com/products/semicon_lab/litewing-drone-positioning-module/)). The drone alone lands at A$113.85 (A$125.23 with GST) and drone plus module at A$190.23 (A$209.25), or A$211.84–233.03 if Tindie charges two first-item rates; no Australian reseller was found, and Elecrow's listing is out of stock ([Core search](https://core-electronics.com.au/search/litewing.md); [Elecrow](https://www.elecrow.com/litewing-esp32-based-programmable-drone.html)). Both store listings say the drone ships without a battery, although CircuitDigest's own battery guide says it comes with one; plan to buy a 1S pack of at least 20C with an MX2.0 (Molex 51005) plug, and because the guide warns there is "no standardisation on MX2.0 connector polarity", check the pack against the board's marking ([CircuitDigest battery guide](https://circuitdigest.com/articles/how-to-select-right-battery-for-litewing)). With no Australian stock, your choices are an AliExpress 1,000 mAh pack from A$4.40 or a re-terminated Phaser GNB 850 mAh. LiteWing charges its pack on board through a TP4056 charger, so it needs no separate charger ([LiteWing wiki](https://circuitdigest.com/wiki/litewing/)).

The **DIY ESP-Drone** route on a printed 8520 frame costs the Flix-class parts plus a hold-sensor set of about **A$48.93** bought mostly locally (Core's PiicoDev VL53L1X, an AliExpress CJMCU-3901 and GY-63) or A$27.09 all from AliExpress at sale prices, half the LiteWing module's delivered cost, but no documented build runs ESP-Drone on loose modules, so you would be the first ([ESP-Drone hardware docs](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/espressif-esp-drone/en/latest/hardware.html)).

### Battery rules limit how LiPos travel, not how big they are

Australia Post's lithium-battery quick-reference guide allows lithium-ion batteries packed by themselves or alongside equipment on **domestic road services only**, with a "Road Transport Only" label; batteries installed in a device may fly, two batteries or four cells at most; and anything over 100 Wh is barred from every service ([AusPost quick reference guide](https://auspost.com.au/content/dam/auspost_corp/media/documents/lithium-batteries-quick-reference-guide.pdf)). Its consumer web page goes further and says lithium batteries should never be packed by themselves, which conflicts with the guide ([AusPost prohibited items](https://auspost.com.au/sending/check-sending-guidelines/dangerous-prohibited-items)). CouriersPlease carries lithium batteries "by road only within Australia" and never by air ([CouriersPlease](https://www.couriersplease.com.au/dangerous-goods)). Internationally, loose lithium-ion batteries may travel only on cargo aircraft at a 30% state of charge ([DHL Australia checklist](https://www.dhl.com/discover/en-au/logistics-advice/import-export-advice/lithium-batteries-dangerous-goods-checklist)), so LiteWing's seller ships the drone without a battery "to go easy on shipping regulations", Elecrow's listing excludes it too, and a search summary of Pyrodrone's battery page says it cannot ship batteries outside the US ([Tindie LiteWing](https://www.tindie.com/products/semicon_lab/litewing-esp32-based-programmable-drone/); [Elecrow](https://www.elecrow.com/litewing-esp32-based-programmable-drone.html); [Pyrodrone](https://pyrodrone.com/collections/gnb-gaoneng-lipo-batteries/products/gaoneng-gnb-1s-300mah-80c-3-8v-hv-li-po-battery-for-whoop-micro-ph2-0-cabled)). Size is no obstacle: a 1S 300 mAh pack holds about 1.1 Wh and a 6S 1,300 mAh pack about 29 Wh, both far under 100 Wh. The obstacle is the road-only rule, which slows battery parcels: Phaser says battery orders take "2–5 business days longer than normal", Core disables express and air services when a LiPo is in the cart, and Phaser never ships batteries abroad ([Phaser delivery](https://phaserfpv.com.au/pages/shipping-delivery); [Core 400 mAh](https://core-electronics.com.au/polymer-lithium-ion-battery-400mah-38456.html); [Phaser battery safety](https://phaserfpv.com.au/pages/battery-safety)). ## Your four filaments cover every part once TPU 85A gets a A$30 hotend

Your P2S needs almost nothing added. It is enclosed, with a 256 mm build cube, a 300 °C hotend, a 110 °C bed, and a hardened-steel nozzle and extruder gear as standard, so it officially prints PLA, PETG, TPU and even carbon-fibre nylons out of the box; it can print PLA and PETG with the cover closed while your room is below 30 °C ([Bambu Wiki P2S FAQ](https://wiki.bambulab.com/en/p2s/manual/p2s-faq)). The exception is TPU 85A, which Bambu supports only on 0.6 and 0.8 mm nozzles, not the 0.4 mm nozzle the P2S ships with, and which no AMS can feed ([Bambu TPU 85A/90A](https://us.store.bambulab.com/products/tpu-85a-tpu-90a)). The cheapest genuine **0.6 mm hardened-steel H2/P2S hotend is A$30 at 3D Printer Gear**, in stock; Phaser has it at A$35.95, which you can add to a drone order, and DREMC's A$28.95 Trianglelab clone has a fixed nozzle ([3D Printer Gear](https://www.3dprintergear.com.au/bambu-lab-hotend-with-hardened-steel-nozzle-0.6-mm); [Phaser hotend](https://phaserfpv.com.au/products/bambu-lab-hotend-h2d); [DREMC](https://store.dremc.com.au/products/bambu-lab-p2s-x2d-hot-end-harden-steel-nano-coating)). Buy two if you will later print carbon-fibre filament, because leftover fibres in a nozzle make TPU harder to feed ([Bambu Wiki TPU guide](https://wiki.bambulab.com/en/filament-acc/filament/tpu-85a-90a-printing-guide-for-p-series)).

Loading TPU 85A is manual: remove the top glass, detach and secure the toolhead's PTFE tube, set the spool beside and above the printer, heat to 250 °C and feed the filament straight into the extruder, pressing Load slowly so it does not wrap the gears; unload manually too, and print at 225 °C on a 30–35 °C bed with Flow Dynamics Calibration off ([Bambu Wiki TPU guide](https://wiki.bambulab.com/en/filament-acc/filament/tpu-85a-90a-printing-guide-for-p-series); [Bambu TPU 85A/90A](https://us.store.bambulab.com/products/tpu-85a-tpu-90a)). Your TPU 65D depends on its maker: if it is Bambu's "TPU for AMS" it is a 68D grade that feeds through the AMS, but a third-party 64–65D filament counts as "generic TPU" and runs from an external spool ([Bambu TPU for AMS](https://us.store.bambulab.com/products/tpu-for-ams); [Bambu AMS HT](https://us.store.bambulab.com/products/ams-ht)), typically at 225–245 °C and only 20–40 mm/s ([KLEMA TPU 65D](https://3ddevice.com.ua/en/product/tpu-65d-filament-klema-1-kg-1-75-mm-black/)). Bambu says TPU must be dried, for 16 h at 90 °C on the P2S bed under a cover or 8 h at 70 °C in a forced-air oven, and recommends 12 h at 60–70 °C for PLA and 75–85 °C for PETG ([Bambu Wiki drying](https://wiki.bambulab.com/en/filament-acc/filament/dry-filament)); its datasheets call kitchen and microwave ovens "not compatible" for drying ([PLA Basic TDS](https://store.bblcdn.com/s1/default/58b85d0f3db94878854a28fdb8a0006e/Bambu_PLA_Basic_Technical_Data_Sheet.pdf)). An AMS HT, A$229 at 3D Printer Gear, can dry TPU 85A but only as a sealed box, not a feeder, so the bed routine is the cheaper path ([3D Printer Gear AMS HT](https://www.3dprintergear.com.au/bambu-lab-ams-ht-automatic-material-system); [Bambu Wiki TPU guide](https://wiki.bambulab.com/en/filament-acc/filament/tpu-85a-90a-printing-guide-for-p-series)).

Bambu's datasheets overturn the usual assumption that PETG is the stronger frame material. PLA is about 40% stiffer (2,580 against 1,810 MPa) and keeps 89% of its strength across layers against PETG's 68%; PETG's advantage is heat, bending under load at 62 °C rather than PLA's 54 °C ([PLA Basic TDS](https://store.bblcdn.com/s1/default/58b85d0f3db94878854a28fdb8a0006e/Bambu_PLA_Basic_Technical_Data_Sheet.pdf); [PETG HF TDS](https://store.bblcdn.com/3a230e260a3a47c2b0db0156e07eef91.pdf)). A car dashboard in the sun reaches 69–71 °C within an hour, so PLA parts should not ride in a parked car on a hot day ([ASU](https://news.asu.edu/20180516-discoveries-asu-study-hot-cars-can-hit-deadly-temperatures-within-one-hour)). TPU 85A absorbs impact, at 124.3 kJ/m² against PLA's 26.6, but is rubber-soft (6.8 MPa) ([TPU 85A TDS](https://cdn.shopify.com/s/files/1/0645/5876/0155/files/Bambu_TPU_85A_Technical_Data_Sheet_a7233668-497b-487f-af8a-3adb49fafaa1.pdf?v=1741314674)), and hard TPU creeps: BASF's 64D keeps 55% of its deformation after 24 h squeezed at 70 °C, so never clamp TPU under motor or stack bolts ([BASF TPU 64D TDS](https://move.forward-am.com/hubfs/AES%20Documentation/Flexible%20Filaments/TPU%2064D/TDS/Ultrafuse_TPU_64D_TDS_EN_v1.1.pdf)).

### Rising NSW battery fires make LiPo discipline non-negotiable

LiPo fires are a growing NSW problem: Fire and Rescue NSW counted at least 318 lithium-ion battery fires in the state in 2024, up from 272 in 2023 and 165 in 2022 ([NSW EPA](https://www.epa.nsw.gov.au/news/epamedia/250210-spate-of-lithium-ion-fires-and-nsw-government-survey-sparks-community-wakeup-call-on-battery-risks)). Treat even a 205 mAh 1S pack with full discipline. A standard LiPo is full at **4.20 V per cell** (4.35 V for high-voltage LiHV packs such as the mylipo spares), should be stored at **3.80–3.85 V** if it will sit for more than about two weeks, should be landed at 3.5–3.6 V and never drained below 3.0 V, and should be charged at 1C or less, with the balance lead connected on multi-cell packs, and never unattended; a puffed pack is permanently damaged ([Oscar Liang LiPo guide](https://oscarliang.com/lipo-battery-guide/)). LiPo bags "slow fires but don't contain them", so if you store packs in a metal ammunition box, remove its rubber seal and drill vent holes (same source). The NSW Government adds that you should use a compatible charger and battery, charge on hard non-flammable surfaces such as concrete or tiles away from doorways and exits, in a room with a working smoke alarm, let batteries cool after use before charging, disconnect once full, and stop using any battery that swells, leaks, cracks or dents; a damaged battery should be kept at least 3 m from structures and combustible materials until disposal ([NSW Government](https://www.nsw.gov.au/housing-and-construction/safety-home/electrical-safety/lithium-ion-batteries-and-e-micromobility-devices)).

Never put a LiPo in any household or kerbside bin. Tape the terminals or bare leads with clear tape, keep spent packs in a ventilated glass jar, and take loose batteries under 5 kg to a free B-cycle drop-off, calling ahead if you have more than 1 kg; NSW EPA says many supermarkets, hardware stores and electronics shops host the bins ([B-cycle FAQs](https://bcycle.com.au/resources/faqs/); [NSW EPA](https://www.epa.nsw.gov.au/your-environment/recycling-and-reuse/never-bin-a-battery)). Swollen or damaged packs need a non-airtight glass container and a call to your council, transfer station or Community Recycling Centre first; NSW has more than 100 free CRCs, open to all residents except at Leichhardt and Cumberland ([NSW EPA CRCs](https://www.epa.nsw.gov.au/Your-environment/Recycling-and-reuse/household-recycling-overview/community-recycling-centres)).

Bench-test every new build in a fixed order: check for shorts with the multimeter, power up through a current limit, test each motor's direction with the props off, test the failsafe with the props off, and only then fit props, matching each to its motor's direction; ArduPilot's own failsafe guide says to remove the propellers whenever a battery is connected for testing ([ArduPilot radio failsafe](https://ardupilot.org/copter/docs/radio-failsafe.html)). On phone-flown drones the failsafe test must cover losing the app: kill the app or the phone's Wi-Fi during a low hover over a soft surface and confirm the drone cuts out or lands. Never spin motors on USB power, use a battery that can supply the motors' current to avoid brownout resets, and on Android fly in airplane mode with Wi-Fi on and VPNs off, because some phones drop the drone while mobile data is enabled ([Flix troubleshooting](https://github.com/okalachev/flix/blob/master/docs/troubleshooting.md); [LiteWing flight guide](https://circuitdigest.com/articles/start-flying-with-litewing)). On the 6S ArduPilot quad, raise the battery failsafe: its 10.5 V default equals 3.5 V per cell only on a 3S pack and would never trigger on 6S before the pack was ruined ([ArduPilot battery failsafe](https://ardupilot.org/copter/docs/failsafe-battery.html)).

## CASA and NSW rules keep phone drones in sight, in daylight and off the big parks

### CASA treats your drones as model aircraft, so rules replace paperwork

A drone flown purely for sport or recreation is legally a **model aircraft**, and model aircraft need **no registration and no operator accreditation at any weight** ([CASR, Part 101](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F1998B00220/2026-06-30/2026-06-30/text/original/epub/OEBPS/document_3/document_3.html); [CASR, Part 47](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F1998B00220/2026-06-30/2026-06-30/text/original/epub/OEBPS/document_2/document_2.html)). A 2026 guide's claim that recreational drones over 250 g must be registered contradicts the regulations in force ([PickDrones](https://pickdrones.com/australia-casa-drone-rules/)). The paperwork returns only if you fly for money: then even a micro drone becomes a remotely piloted aircraft needing registration and accreditation (CASR, Parts 47 and 101, as above). Because casa.gov.au could not be loaded, CASA's plain-English rules come from a retailer's verbatim copy: fly no higher than 120 m (400 ft); keep at least 30 m from other people; fly one drone at a time; keep it within visual line of sight, "always being able to see the drone with your own eyes (rather than through a device, screen or goggles)"; do not fly over people or populous areas such as "beaches, parks, events, or sport ovals where there is a game in progress"; fly only by day and not through cloud or fog; keep away from emergency operations; and respect privacy ([Sphere Drones reproduction](https://www.spheredrones.com.au/resources/blog/drone-safety-rules)). Watching the phone's camera feed instead of the drone therefore breaches the line-of-sight rule, an offence carrying 50 penalty units, A$16,500 at the A$330 unit value before its 1 July 2026 indexation ([CASR, Part 101](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F1998B00220/2026-06-30/2026-06-30/text/original/epub/OEBPS/document_3/document_3.html); [Crimes Act 1914, s 4AA](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C1914A00012/2026-08-27/2026-08-27/text/original/epub/OEBPS/document_1/document_1.html)).

| Rule | 250 g or less: ESP-FLY (25 g), Flix (~66 g), LiteWing, whoops | Over 250 g: printed 5-inch GPS quad |
|---|---|---|
| Registration, accreditation, Remote ID | None | None |
| Height and sight | ≤120 m; your own eyes on the drone | Same |
| Night | No lawful option outdoors: the night ban yields only to MAAA procedures for models over 250 g | Only under MAAA written procedures |
| People and populous areas | CASA's rule says 30 m and no overflight; the regulation binds only heavier models | 30 m and populous-area limits are law |
| Controlled-airport no-fly zone (3 NM ≈ 5.5 km, corridors to 8.5 km) | Allowed only outside the runway approach and departure corridors and off airport land, and below 45 m anywhere within 3 NM | Prohibited |
| Restricted areas, including Sydney Harbour | Prohibited without permission | Prohibited |
| FPV goggles outdoors | Only at MAAA fields and events, with an observer | Same |
| Indoors (whole roof and walls in place) | Only the "don't create a hazard" rule applies | Same |

The table condenses the regulations and the Part 101 Manual of Standards ([CASR, Part 101](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F1998B00220/2026-06-30/2026-06-30/text/original/epub/OEBPS/document_3/document_3.html); [Part 101 MOS](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2019L00593/2024-04-30/2024-04-30/text/original/epub/OEBPS/document_1/document_1.html)). Two points deserve detail. The sub-250 g airport allowance rests on MOS 4.03(6), and the approach and departure paths it excludes run from the ground up to 7 km beyond each runway end, from 90 m between 7 and 8.5 km, and from 45 m (150 ft, measured from aerodrome elevation) anywhere within 3 NM, so on high ground near an airport your usable ceiling is lower (same MOS). The only recreational approval for outdoor FPV found is CASA.RPAS.0572, which lets MAAA affiliate members and defined visitors fly beyond their own sight "only at registered MAAA club flying fields or CASA approved MAAA events" until the end of August 2027 ([CASA.RPAS.0572](https://www.maaa.asn.au/images/Drones/MAAA_FPV_approval.pdf)); MAAA policy then requires two members, with an observer who keeps the model in sight and can take over by handing over the radio ([MAAA MOP066](https://www.maaa.asn.au/images/MOP066-Policy-FPV-and-SGMA-Policy_190819.pdf)). A friend acting as spotter in a public park does not make goggle flying legal. Remote ID is not required; the Government expects a mandate "by 2030, subject to a regulatory impact analysis", and a secondary claim of a 2027 rollout has no primary source ([Unmanned Airspace](https://www.unmannedairspace.info/emerging-regulations/australia-to-reform-airspace-to-better-accommodate-drones-by-2030/)).

### Sydney's airspace changed on 9 July 2026

Since 9 July 2026 the Sydney Basin has **four control zones side by side**: Western Sydney International (Class C), Sydney Kingsford Smith (Class C, with redefined boundaries), and Bankstown and Camden (both Class D), with Bankstown's zone extending north-west ([Australian Flying](https://www.australianflying.com.au/latest/sydney-basin-airspace-overhaul-takes-effect-9-july-what-pilots-need-to-do-now)). All four are controlled aerodromes, so each carries the 5.5 km no-fly zone and runway corridors in the table above; whether Bankstown's and Camden's zones lapse outside tower hours was not checked, so trust the app on the day. Western Sydney International took freight from 26 July 2026, opens to passengers on 25 October, and is intended to open for night movements on 1 November ([Prime Minister](https://www.pm.gov.au/media/its-official-western-sydney-open-passengers-25-october-and-freight-26-july-2026); [Airservices](https://engage.airservicesaustralia.com/western-sydney-airport/news_feed/wsi-opening-dates-announced)), so maps and app caches from before July understate the no-fly areas in western Sydney. Sydney Harbour sits under restricted areas R407A and R407B from the same date, and CASA's permission covers only licensed remote pilots doing aerial work, so hobbyists have no standing permission there; flying in a restricted area without permission is a strict-liability offence of 25 penalty units ([CASA 28/26](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2026L00946/asmade/2026-07-08/text/original/pdf); [explanatory statement](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2026L00946/asmade/2026-07-08/es/original/pdf)). Helicopter landing sites carry a 0.75 NM no-fly cylinder up to 400 ft, and near them or uncontrolled aerodromes you must move away and land if a manned aircraft appears, although a sub-250 g model may keep flying outside the movement area and approach paths ([Part 101 MOS](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2019L00593/2024-04-30/2024-04-30/text/original/epub/OEBPS/document_1/document_1.html)). Check a CASA-verified app before every flight; Wing's OpenSky is free and builds a flight brief for recreational flyers ([OpenSky](https://wing.com/opensky); [CASA verified apps](https://www.casa.gov.au/droneapp)). The drone status of RAAF Base Richmond, Holsworthy and hospital helipads was not established from primary sources, so rely on the app there.

### Parks, councils and privacy law close most of the obvious spots

Land managers add a second layer. NSW National Parks requires written approval at least 10 days ahead to launch, land or fly in any park it manages, some parks refuse recreational drones, and drones are prohibited in parks close to controlled aerodromes such as Sydney Airport ([NSW National Parks](https://www.nationalparks.nsw.gov.au/conservation-and-heritage/care-for-parks/drones-in-parks); [NPWS policy](https://www.environment.nsw.gov.au/topics/parks-reserves-and-protected-areas/park-policies/drones-in-parks)). Recreational drones are prohibited on all Greater Sydney Parklands land, which covers Centennial Parklands, Parramatta Park, Western Sydney Parklands, Callan Park and Fernhill Estate ([Greater Sydney Parklands](https://www.greatersydneyparklands.nsw.gov.au/use-drones-greater-sydney-parklands-sites)), and on Botanic Gardens of Sydney land, which covers the Royal Botanic Garden, the Domain, Mount Annan and Mount Tomah ([Botanic Gardens guideline](https://www.botanicgardens.org.au/sites/default/files/2025-04/ABG%20-%20Use-of-drones.pdf)). The City of Sydney "does not approve drone use for non-commercial purposes" on its land ([City of Sydney drone guidelines](https://www.cityofsydney.nsw.gov.au/-/media/corporate/files/services/business-permits-approvals-tenders/apply-for-a-filming-or-photography-permit/drone-guidelines-march-2025.pdf?download=true)), and Northern Beaches Council has warned that flying over populated areas such as beaches without CASA authorisation is illegal ([Northern Beaches Council](https://www.northernbeaches.nsw.gov.au/council/news/media-releases/drones-to-keep-sydneys-beaches-and-parks-covid-safe)). Other councils regulate through park signage and plans of management, which this research did not verify park by park.

Any camera you fit brings privacy law into play. Recording activities on premises with an optical surveillance device without the owner's or occupier's consent is an offence under the NSW Surveillance Devices Act 2007 carrying up to five years' imprisonment, although that offence turns on entering the premises ([NSW Communities and Justice](https://dcj.nsw.gov.au/content/dam/dcj/dcj-website/documents/legal-and-justice/laws-and-legislation/surveillance-devices-act-2007-and-statutory-powers-to-make-audio-and-video-recordings.pdf)); filming a person's private act for sexual gratification without consent is a separate crime ([Sydney Criminal Lawyers](https://sydneycriminallawyers.com.au/?p=47959)). Since 10 June 2025 a federal statutory tort also lets people sue for serious invasions of privacy, explicitly including "recording the person's private activities", with no need to prove damage ([Privacy Act 1988, Schedule 2](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A03712/2026-06-04/2026-06-04/text/original/epub/OEBPS/document_1/document_1.html)), so a camera micro hovering near a neighbour's yard creates civil exposure even where the criminal offence would not apply.

### Radio rules let Wi-Fi and ELRS run freely but cap analog video at 25 mW

The Low Interference Potential Devices Class Licence 2025, in force since 1 October 2025, governs every transmitter here ([LIPD 2025](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2025L01047/asmade/2025-09-05/text/original/epub/OEBPS/document_1/document_1.html)). The ESP32 drones' Wi-Fi and ESP-NOW links fall in its 2.4 GHz digital-modulation class, allowed up to 4 W EIRP, far above any ESP32 module's output; ExpressLRS's ISM_2400 mode hops across 80 channels and so qualifies for the 4 W frequency-hopping class, but a 1 W radio into an 8 dBi patch antenna reaches about 6.3 W EIRP and breaks it (same licence; [ExpressLRS FHSS source](https://github.com/ExpressLRS/ExpressLRS/blob/master/src/lib/FHSS/FHSS.cpp)). For 900 MHz ExpressLRS choose AU_915, which hops 915.5–926.9 MHz within the 1 W band, because FCC_915 transmits down to 903.5 MHz, outside it ([ExpressLRS firmware options](https://www.expresslrs.org/quick-start/firmware-options/)). Analog 5.8 GHz video, including the cameras on whoops and the 5-inch build, falls under the general class: **25 mW EIRP, with the whole signal inside 5,725–5,875 MHz**, so popular channels such as Raceband 1 (5,658 MHz), 2 (5,695), 7 (5,880) and 8 (5,917) are out at any power and the licence-free setting is 25 mW on in-band channels ([LIPD 2025](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2025L01047/asmade/2025-09-05/text/original/epub/OEBPS/document_1/document_1.html)). Whether DJI, Walksnail and HDZero digital systems qualify for the 4 W digital class is unclear without ACMA guidance. A Standard or Advanced amateur licence opens 2.40–2.45 and 5.65–5.85 GHz at higher power, but only for "amateur purposes", with no financial gain and your call sign sent at least every 10 minutes ([Amateur Stations Class Licence 2023](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2023L01648/asmade/2023-12-12/text/original/epub/OEBPS/document_1/document_1.html)); club assessors put the one-off cost at about A$48 for the ACMA certificate and A$32 for a call sign ([AHARS](https://ahars.au/get-licensed/)).

### Indoors, private land and MAAA fields are where you can legally fly

| Venue | Status for your drones |
|---|---|
| Indoors: home, a hired hall or sports centre, with permission | Legal for every stage; only the hazard rule applies, and FPV indoors needs no CASA approval ([CASR 101.005](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F1998B00220/2026-06-30/2026-06-30/text/original/epub/OEBPS/document_3/document_3.html); [CASA FPV page](https://www.casa.gov.au/drones/drone-rules/flying-first-person-view)) |
| Private land with the owner's permission, outside R407 and (for drones over 250 g) airport no-fly zones | Legal under CASA's rules; check the app |
| MAAA club field | Legal, including goggles with an observer; the natural home for the 5-inch GPS quad ([CASA.RPAS.0572](https://www.maaa.asn.au/images/Drones/MAAA_FPV_approval.pdf)) |
| NSW national parks | Written NPWS approval at least 10 days ahead; banned near Sydney Airport |
| Centennial Parklands, Parramatta Park, Western Sydney Parklands, Callan Park, Fernhill | Prohibited |
| Royal Botanic Garden, the Domain, Mount Annan, Mount Tomah | Prohibited |
| City of Sydney council land | No non-commercial approvals |
| Sydney Harbour (R407A/B) | No hobby permission |
| Beaches and busy parks | Populous areas: CASA says do not overfly people; Northern Beaches calls it illegal |
| Other council parks | Check signage and plans of management, then CASA's 30 m and populous-area rules |

The MAAA, which CASA recognises as the administrator of model flying, has more than 9,000 members in more than 330 clubs and sells itself on FPV access and insurance "with no excess" ([MAAA MOP066](https://www.maaa.asn.au/images/MOP066-Policy-FPV-and-SGMA-Policy_190819.pdf); [MAAA why join](https://maaa.asn.au/aircraft/why-join)). Its insurance policy text is less generous: members contribute A$500 towards a public-liability claim's excess, cover excludes "UAVs" as MAAA defines them, and its FAQ gives conflicting answers on whether flying in a local park with the owner's permission is covered ([MAAA MOP057](https://www.maaa.asn.au/images/2024/MOP057-Policy-Insurance-Conditions.pdf); [MAAA FAQ](https://maaa.asn.au/membership/faqs)), so ask the association before relying on it for a GPS quad. Sydney options include Western Sydney Model Aviators' 16-hectare field at Vineyard ([WSMA](https://wsma.au/)) and community FPV groups at Forestville (Northern Beaches Drone Crew), Shalvey (Outer Heaven) and the Menai Indoor Sports Centre (Sydney Drone Club) ([Rising Sun FPV club directory](https://risingsunfpv.com/blogs/helpful-guides/fpv-drone-clubs-australia-find-your-local-club)). Their MAAA affiliation and drone policies were not verified, and outside buildings only registered MAAA fields and approved MAAA events make goggle flying lawful, so ask each group whether it is MAAA-affiliated and whether its field is registered.

## Conclusion

Sydney's rules reinforce the micro-first path rather than merely tolerating it. With four controlled airports, each ringed by a 5.5 km no-fly zone and runway corridors reaching 8.5 km, plus the harbour's restricted areas, and with only models of 250 g or less allowed inside those no-fly zones at all, the 25–66 g phone-flown micros are not just the cheapest drones you can build here but the ones you may legally fly in the most places, while the 600 g GPS quad belongs at an MAAA field, the same place where outdoor goggle flying becomes lawful. The printer's economics invert in the same direction: your first drone arrives pre-printed and a printed whoop frame saves nothing, so the P2S pays for itself as a repair shop for frames no store was found selling, as the only factory for Flix's airframe, and as the source of a A$3 PETG frame where a 5-inch would otherwise need A$70–94 of carbon.

The decisions that matter most are therefore logistical and legal, not technical. Two purchases outlast their stage, the HexaCharger (stages 1 and 3) and the RadioMaster Pocket (stage 3's radio is stage 4's safety net), and both sit beside the batteries you cannot sensibly import at Phaser's counter in Somersby. The rules are also moving: Western Sydney International is due to open for night movements on 1 November 2026, MAAA's FPV approval lapses at the end of August 2027 unless renewed, and a Remote ID mandate is targeted for 2030. Treat a fresh check of a CASA-verified app as part of every pre-flight, because Sydney's drone map has already been redrawn once this year.
