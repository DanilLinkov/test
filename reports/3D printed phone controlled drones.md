# Tiny ESP32 Quads Turn Phones Into Transmitters

The most direct way to 3D print a drone and fly it from your phone is an ESP32 micro-quadcopter that creates its own Wi-Fi network, so an app on your phone becomes the transmitter and your printer makes a frame of 4–20 g. Only two printable designs pair a phone app with solid evidence that people have built and flown them. **ESP-FLY** is a 25 g, 50 mm quad flown with Espressif's free ESP-Drone app and sold as a **$59.99 Seeed kit** whose fiddly surface-mount circuit board comes pre-assembled, while **Flix** is an open-source quad you hand-solder from an ESP32 dev board, a motion-sensor (IMU) breakout and four transistors (MOSFETs) onto a printed frame and fly from **Android only** with QGroundControl or Mavlink Joystick. Both fly about five minutes, reach roughly 50 m over Wi-Fi and only keep themselves level: **holding altitude adds a barometer or distance sensor costing under $20, holding position indoors adds a $20–55 optical-flow sensor, and GPS features (outdoor position hold, return-to-home, tap-to-fly, waypoint missions) need a different machine**—a printed 5-inch ArduPilot quad costing about $507 including radio, batteries and charger, or about $685 with optical flow, Remote ID and HD video to the phone. You command that quad from QGroundControl on Android, and ArduPilot's documentation says to keep a handheld radio transmitter as backup. The most popular printable designs, led by the BM Aether 4 FPV frame with 12,834 downloads and 4,291 prints, are built to be flown with a radio, so on them the phone is a video screen and setup tool rather than a joystick. Your filaments cover nearly every printed part—PLA or PETG for micro frames, PETG or stronger for larger arms, TPU 65D for mounts and skids, TPU 85A for soft bumpers and pads—although TPU 85A needs a $20.99 0.6 mm hotend and hand feeding on the P2S. Budget about $100 for tools beyond your soldering station, treat even tiny single-cell (1S) batteries with full LiPo safety discipline, and keep the drone under 100 g (UK) or 250 g (US, EU, Canada) to avoid most registration, Remote ID and training rules. An Android phone opens far more of this ecosystem than an iPhone, whose drone apps are older, fewer or missing.

## Phone control, not printing, decides which drone you can build

Your P2S is not the bottleneck. It is an enclosed printer with a 256 mm build cube, a 300 °C hotend, a 110 °C bed and a hardened-steel nozzle and extruder gear as standard. That means it officially prints everything from PLA to the carbon-fibre nylons that serious frames call for ([Bambu Wiki P2S FAQ](https://wiki.bambulab.com/en/p2s/manual/p2s-faq)). The bottleneck is the link between phone and drone.

A sweep of Bambu's MakerWorld catalogue on 5 October 2026 returned 2,974 unique drone-related designs. None came from Bambu itself, and the BM Aether 4 FPV frame led the field. Yet the **only design ready to fly from a phone was a free ESP-FLY frame remix with 423 downloads** ([Bambu search API](https://api.bambulab.com/v1/search-service/select/design2?keyword=drone&limit=50&offset=0); [MakerWorld ESP-FLY remix](https://makerworld.com/en/models/1530477-esp-fly-frame-drone-housing)). Printables shows the same split. Its most-downloaded frame, ProgrammaDan's Goblin, has 7,664 downloads, while its only ESP32 camera-drone listing has 35 downloads and no files yet ([Printables Goblin](https://www.printables.com/model/396395-goblin-fpv-drone); [Printables esp32 cam drone](https://www.printables.com/model/947634-esp32-cam-drone)). Phone-flown designs live on GitHub and YouTube instead, where Max Imagination's ESP-FLY build video has passed **1.63 million views** ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=V_mZsiZcy7s)).

Since you are new to drones, it helps to know that every multirotor, phone-flown or not, is built from the same six subsystems. The class of drone you choose decides which version of each you buy. The flight controller carries the IMU (inertial measurement unit), a gyroscope plus accelerometer that keeps the craft level. Motor drivers switch battery current to four motors that spin as two clockwise and two counter-clockwise pairs. A battery, a control link and a frame complete the minimum, and optional sensors add features. The table below shows what each subsystem looks like in the three classes that matter here.

| Subsystem | Phone-native ESP32 micro | Brushless FPV quad (whoop to 5") | GPS/autonomous quad (5" and up) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flight controller and IMU | ESP32-S3 board such as the XIAO ($7.49) plus an MPU6050, or an integrated board ([Seeed](https://www.seeedstudio.com/XIAO-ESP32S3-p-5627.html)) | All-in-one (AIO) whoop board: ICM-42688-P gyro, ESC, ELRS receiver and video transmitter on one board, $45–55 ([BetaFPV](https://betafpv.com/products/air-brushless-flight-controller)) | H7 flight controller running ArduPilot, e.g. MicoAir743 V2 at $72.99 ([Pyrodrone](https://pyrodrone.com/collections/micoair-products/products/micoair-h743-v2-2-6s-flight-controller-with-bluetooth-and-pre-loaded-with-ardupilot-30-30mm)) |
| Motor drivers | Four logic-level MOSFETs such as SI2302 or UMW 100N03A ([CircuitDigest](https://circuitdigest.com/microcontroller-projects/DIY-wifi-controlled-drone); [Flix](https://github.com/okalachev/flix)) | ESC built into the AIO board, or a separate 4-in-1 ESC running Bluejay/AM32 firmware | 4-in-1 ESC, $44–58 ([RDQ](https://www.racedayquads.com/products/tbs-lucid-60a-8s-am32-4-in-1-esc)) |
| Motors and props | 615/716/720/8520 coreless brushed motors with 30–65 mm props | 0702/0802 (1S whoop), 1404 (3") or 2207 (5") brushless | 2207 (5") or 2806.5 (7") brushless |
| Battery | 1S 3.7 V LiPo, 150–1,300 mAh | 1S BT2.0 packs, or 4S–6S packs with XT30/XT60 plugs | 6S LiPo or Li-ion |
| Control link | Drone's own Wi-Fi to a phone app | ELRS receiver plus a handheld radio | ELRS radio, plus MAVLink telemetry over Bluetooth, Wi-Fi or SiK radio to a phone ground-station app |
| Frame | Printed (4–20 g), or the circuit board itself | Printed or carbon | Printed PETG or fibre-filled filament, or carbon |

These three classes, plus a set of unconventional shortcuts, make up the four routes to your goal.

| Route | Representative designs | What the phone does | What you print | Features | Per-drone cost | Beginner fit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Phone-native ESP32 micro | ESP-FLY, Flix, ESP-Drone on a printed frame, LiteWing | Full stick control over the drone's Wi-Fi | Whole frame, or guards on circuit-board-frame designs | Self-levelling; height and position hold with add-on sensors on ESP-Drone and LiteWing | ≈$30–60 DIY; $49–60 kits | Best starting point |
| Printed brushless FPV | BM Aether 4, Nano Long Range, Goblin, printed whoops | Video (Android), configuration, simulator; sticks only through experimental bridges | Frames, ducts, canopies, mounts | Manual and acrobatic flight, FPV video | ≈$145 (whoop) to ≈$502 (5" with batteries and charger) | Needs a $33–72 radio |
| Printed GPS/autonomous | Grasshopper5, Teardrop Quad, Sub-250 g Autonomous Platform | Ground station: tap-to-fly, orbit, follow-me, missions, return-to-home | Frame, GPS mast, mounts | GPS position hold, return-to-home, missions, optical-flow hold | ≈$507–685 | Advanced; radio backup advised |
| Unconventional | Toy-drone transplant, used Tello with printed shell, Pluto X, blimp, PowerUp plane | Ranges from full control to video only | Shells, guards, gondolas | Varies | $15–60; Pluto X ₹14,000–20,000 | Niche or dead ends |

Each route uses the phone differently for a reason. Phone Wi-Fi reaches only about 50 m on these builds. The author of an ESP32 phone bridge for full-size flight controllers warns that "WiFi was not designed for real time robust control applications" ([Michal Schwarz](https://michalschwarz.github.io/rc-controller/esp32/quadcopter/f450/asgard32/schema/2019/05/07/esp32-drone-v1.0.0.html)). That weakness is tolerable on a 25 g indoor quad whose worst failure is a bump. It is not tolerable on a printed 600 g 5-inch that its designer clocks at 200 km/h ([MakerWorld Hydrance](https://makerworld.com/en/models/2573757-200-km-h-3d-printed-drone-for-under-200-cad)), which is why those designs assume a dedicated radio.

## Two printable ESP32 quads pair phone apps with real flight evidence

### ESP-FLY puts a phone-flown quad in 25 grams

ESP-FLY, created by Max Imagination and sold as a Seeed Studio kit since 2026, is the closest thing to a turnkey answer to your question. It is a **50 mm-class quad (67 × 67 × 31 mm with props) weighing 18 g without a battery and 25 g with its 250 mAh 1S LiPo**. Four 615 coreless motors produce about 17 g of thrust each. The brain is a Seeed XIAO ESP32-S3 stacked on a custom four-layer IMU and motor-driver board, which carries an MPU-6050 motion sensor and four SI2300 MOSFETs. It flies about 5 minutes (5.5 in testing) and reaches roughly **50 m over Wi-Fi or 200 m over ESP-NOW**, Espressif's direct radio link between ESP32 chips. It offers only Angle and Acro modes. There is no altitude hold, GPS or autonomy, and the recommended payload is just 3 g ([Seeed ESP-FLY repo](https://github.com/Seeed-Projects/Co-Create_ESP-FLY)).

The drone creates its own Wi-Fi access point (password 12345678), and you fly it with Espressif's ESP-Drone app. The app offers on-screen sticks or phone-tilt control, plus trims and a yaw lock. On iPhone it comes from the App Store. On Android it is an APK file from the Chinese hosting site pgyer.com, so you have to allow installs from unknown apps ([Instructables](https://www.instructables.com/Build-the-Smallest-ESP32-Drone-You-Can-Fly-With-Yo/)).

The kit costs **$59.99** ($49.99 each for ten or more). It includes the XIAO, the pre-assembled IMU/motor module, motors, eight props, the battery and a printed frame set with a standard cover and an FPV-camera cover. You supply a soldering iron, superglue, a prop remover and a USB-C cable. Seeed describes assembly as "basic soldering" suitable for beginners ([Seeed product page](https://www.seeedstudio.com/ESP-FLY-co-create-p-6744.html); [Seeed ESP-FLY repo](https://github.com/Seeed-Projects/Co-Create_ESP-FLY)).

Building from scratch is cheaper on paper: **$37.30 ±$10** for the XIAO ($7.49), the MPU6050 ($4.40), motors ($10.99), a LiPo ($9.99) and a $0.42 JLCPCB circuit board. But assembling that board needs an SMD stencil and a reflow hot plate, which you do not own, and the original Instructable rates the from-scratch route for intermediate or advanced makers ([Elektor Labs](https://www.elektormagazine.com/labs/esp-fly-the-smallest-esp32-drone-you-can-build); [Instructables](https://www.instructables.com/Build-the-Smallest-ESP32-Drone-You-Can-Fly-With-Yo/)).

For your printer there are two frame options. The author's 4 g frame STL is a paid Cults3D download. It is printed in ePLA at 0.12 mm layers, 10% infill and supports on, with a 220 °C nozzle and 60 °C bed, and takes about 30 minutes. A free MakerWorld remix by @Bennni claims to be 2–3 g lighter and turns the battery holder into the landing foot. It ships a P2S-listed PLA profile (0.08 mm layers, 2.3 h) and has 139 recorded prints and a 4.75★ rating from four raters ([Cults3D](https://cults3d.com/en/3d-model/gadget/esp-fly-an-esp32-micro-drone-body-frame-3d-design-stl-files); [Instructables](https://www.instructables.com/Build-the-Smallest-ESP32-Drone-You-Can-Fly-With-Yo/); [MakerWorld remix](https://makerworld.com/en/models/1530477-esp-fly-frame-drone-housing)).

The evidence that ESP-FLY flies is broad. The main build video has 1.63 million views and the Instructable 46,489. The author reports a 2.7:1 thrust-to-weight ratio and flights out to about 50 m "before losing sight" ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=V_mZsiZcy7s); [Instructables](https://www.instructables.com/Build-the-Smallest-ESP32-Drone-You-Can-Fly-With-Yo/)). Three cautions apply. First, the remix's comments ask about longer legs for thicker batteries and about other motors, but contain no explicit flight report ([MakerWorld comments](https://api.bambulab.com/v1/comment-service/commentandrating?designId=1530477&offset=0&limit=20&type=0)). Second, battery advice conflicts: 150–450 mAh in the Instructable, 150–350 mAh in the kit FAQ. Third, the "7–25 ms" latency and "100 m+" range figures in the Elektor write-up are unmeasured claims, and they conflict with Seeed's 50 m ([Elektor Labs](https://www.elektormagazine.com/labs/esp-fly-the-smallest-esp32-drone-you-can-build); [Seeed ESP-FLY repo](https://github.com/Seeed-Projects/Co-Create_ESP-FLY)).

### Flix trades convenience for a fully open, hand-built design

Flix, by Oleg Kalachev, is the strongest choice if you want to print the whole airframe and wire everything yourself from off-the-shelf modules. The repository has 2,044 stars and was last updated on 30 September 2026. It ships the **frame as STL and STEP files**, plus an ESP32 holder and printed washers. It also includes firmware of under 2,000 lines of Arduino code, MAVLink communication over Wi-Fi or ESP-NOW, a Gazebo simulator and a Python library ([Flix README](https://github.com/okalachev/flix)).

The parts list is specific:

| Part | What to buy |
|---|---|
| Controller | An ESP32 Mini board (ESP32-S3 and C3 boards also work) |
| Motion sensor (IMU) | Preferably a GY-91 breakout, which the frame is shaped for; MPU-9265, ICM-20948 and GY-521 boards also work |
| Motors and props | **Four 8520 brushed motors rated at exactly 3.7 V**, not "3.7–6 V" types, with 55 or 65 mm props |
| Motor drivers | Four UMW 100N03A MOSFETs ("don't use KIA 100N03A") with 10 kΩ pull-down resistors |
| Battery | A 1S LiPo of at least 1,000 mAh and 25C, with an MX2.0 connector |
| Extras | Optional 5 V boost converter; M3 and M1.4 screws |

Beyond the tools you own, you need screwdrivers and a multimeter. The frame prints at 0.2 mm layers with 100% infill ([Flix README](https://github.com/okalachev/flix)).

Phone flight with Flix is Android-only. You join the drone's `flix` network (password `flixwifi`) and fly with either the Mavlink Joystick app, an APK released in 2026, or QGroundControl's virtual joystick with Auto-Center Throttle turned off. The docs advise lowering the `CTL_ATT_MAX` parameter (which caps the tilt angle) when flying from a phone ([Flix usage docs](https://github.com/okalachev/flix/blob/master/docs/usage.md); [Mavlink Joystick](https://github.com/goldarte/mavlink-joystick)). QGroundControl publishes no iOS build ([QGC download docs](https://docs.qgroundcontrol.com/master/en/qgc-user-guide/getting_started/download_and_install.html)).

The main STAB mode keeps the drone level but "doesn't stabilize its position, so slight drift is possible". Altitude hold is not in the main firmware (the barometer is "not used for now"), and position control is still in development. At RoboCamp in July 2026, several students added altitude hold, and two achieved stable position hold using distance sensors and an overhead camera ([Flix usage docs](https://github.com/okalachev/flix/blob/master/docs/usage.md); [Flix user builds](https://github.com/okalachev/flix/blob/master/docs/user.md)).

The author is candid that Flix is "not easy to assemble and set up", with "no guarantee that it will work perfectly, or even work at all" ([Flix README](https://github.com/okalachev/flix)). The documented failure causes are wrong motor voltage, the wrong IMU model or orientation, a battery that cannot deliver about 15 A (at least 15C for a 1,000 mAh pack), loosely mounted motors ("any vibration may lead to bad attitude estimation"), and firewalls or VPNs that block QGroundControl ([Flix troubleshooting](https://github.com/okalachev/flix/blob/master/docs/troubleshooting.md); [Flix assembly](https://github.com/okalachev/flix/blob/master/docs/assembly.md)).

Its user gallery shows a 66 g build on a QX95 frame, an ESP32-S3-CAM build that streams video, and classroom use. The demo videos have 38,899 and 18,935 views ([Flix user builds](https://github.com/okalachev/flix/blob/master/docs/user.md); [YouTube Flix v0](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8GzzIQ3C6DQ); [YouTube Flix v1](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hT46CZ1CgC4)).

One legal wrinkle: the repository has no licence file. Default copyright therefore governs remixing or redistributing the frame, even though a derivative project describes Flix as MIT-licensed ([Sprig-Drone](https://github.com/Frapais/Sprig-Drone)). There is no official cost. Adding up cited prices for an ESP32 board (a XIAO is $7.49), an MPU6050, a coreless motor set, props, MOSFETs and one battery gives roughly **$30–60**. That matches CircuitDigest's $30–50 for a comparable brushed ESP32 drone ([Seeed XIAO](https://www.seeedstudio.com/XIAO-ESP32S3-p-5627.html); [CircuitDigest](https://circuitdigest.com/microcontroller-projects/DIY-wifi-controlled-drone)).

### ESP-Drone, LiteWing and a long tail of smaller projects

**ESP-Drone.** Espressif's ESP-Drone firmware (2,219 stars, GPL-3.0 licence) underpins both ESP-FLY and LiteWing, and it is the most capable phone-flown firmware. It runs on ESP32, S2 and S3 chips and offers Stabilize, Height-hold and Position-hold modes. The two hold modes need extension boards: a PMW3901 optical-flow sensor plus a VL53L1X time-of-flight distance sensor, an MS5611 barometer and an HMC5883 compass ([ESP-Drone README](https://github.com/espressif/esp-drone); [ESP-Drone hardware docs](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/espressif-esp-drone/en/latest/hardware.html)). Espressif has offered only "limited support" since December 2022. Its reference drone uses the circuit board as the frame, although the older ESPlane-FC-V1 board "requires external drone frame assembly", which is a precedent for putting ESP-Drone electronics on a printed frame.

Printable frame candidates exist: Printables' Brushed Drone Frame for 8.5 mm motors (1,157 downloads), its Micro Quad (Whoop) Brushed frame for 8520 motors (796 downloads; 0.2 mm layers, 4 walls, 100% infill), an 18 g, 110 mm "Esp32 Drone" frame in PLA on MakerWorld, and a CC0 Thingiverse frame for ESP-WROOM-32 boards ([Printables Brushed Drone Frame](https://www.printables.com/model/839744-brushed-drone-frame); [Printables Micro Quad](https://www.printables.com/model/779536-micro-quad-whoop-brushed-drone-frame); [MakerWorld Esp32 Drone](https://makerworld.com/en/models/2580472-esp32-drone); [Thingiverse](https://www.thingiverse.com/thing:5890481)). None of these has been validated with ESP-Drone by its designer, and no documented build runs ESP-Drone on loose modules rather than a custom board, so on this route you would be a pioneer.

**LiteWing.** CircuitDigest's LiteWing is the easiest phone-flown drone that offers hold modes, at the cost of printability: its 100 × 100 mm FR4 circuit board is the frame, so your printer contributes only guards, skids and pods. The current ESP32-S3 version weighs about 45 g without a battery and carries about 25 g of payload on 720 coreless motors. It sells assembled for **$49 without a battery** ([Tindie](https://www.tindie.com/products/semicon_lab/litewing-esp32-based-programmable-drone/)). An optional positioning module adds VL53L1X height hold and PMW3901 position hold, both listed as "tested and working", with an MS5611 barometer "coming soon" ([LiteWing wiki](https://circuitdigest.com/wiki/litewing/)). LiteWing's own app, launched in September 2025, adds a height-hold mode, battery warnings, emergency stop and gradual landing, and claims support for other drones using the Crazyflie protocol ([CircuitDigest](https://circuitdigest.com/articles/litewing-esp32-drone-gets-new-mobile-app)). The original 2024 design, by CircuitDigest's numbers, cost $30–50 to build and weighed about 60 g. It flew 5–7 minutes on a 1,300 mAh battery, reached 30–50 m outdoors and 15–25 m indoors, and drifted in winds above 5–8 mph ([CircuitDigest](https://circuitdigest.com/microcontroller-projects/DIY-wifi-controlled-drone)).

**Smaller projects.** A long tail of smaller projects shows that the "ESP32 hotspot plus app" idea is easy to copy but hard to mature. **PicoW Copter** pairs a Raspberry Pi Pico W with a printed PLA frame and an Android UDP Joystick app for $25–30, but has been inactive since May 2024 ([PicoW_Copter](https://github.com/anish-natekar/PicoW_Copter); [PicoW docs](https://picow-copter-docs.readthedocs.io/en/latest/)). **Pro Know's popsicle-stick ESP32 drone**, whose video has 48,862 views, links to a printed version on Cults3D ([GitHub](https://github.com/proknowdiy/esp32_drone)). **Comet Drone, cifertech's ESP32-Drone and the Chinese CF-Drone** (255 stars) fly from a phone's web browser with nothing to install, and Comet cuts its motors after 500 ms without Wi-Fi ([Comet-Drone](https://github.com/CoreCometIndustries/Comet-Drone); [cifertech](https://github.com/cifertech/ESP32-Drone); [CF-Drone](https://github.com/songge8/CF-Drone)). **01Studio's pyDrone** adds a barometer, a compass, MicroPython programming and an Android app, and **Jon VB's Hackaday.io drone** streamed an ArduCAM camera to an Android controller as early as 2022 ([pyDrone](https://github.com/01studio-lab/pyDrone); [Hackaday.io](https://hackaday.io/project/188578-esp32-drone)).

**Near misses.** Four well-known platforms fall just outside your brief. **M5Stack's StampFly** has altitude and position hold but flies from an ESP-NOW joystick rather than a phone ([stampfly_ecosystem](https://github.com/M5Fly-kanazawa/stampfly_ecosystem)). **ESP-FC** firmware uses Wi-Fi only for configuration and refuses to arm the motors while Wi-Fi is on ([ESP-FC wireless docs](https://github.com/rtlopez/esp-fc/blob/master/docs/wireless.md)). **Bitcraze's Crazyflie 2.1+** flies well from iOS and Android over Bluetooth, but costs $240 and only its accessories are printable ([Bitcraze store](https://store.bitcraze.io/collections/kits)). **Jonas Gartner's "DIY Drone Frame & Remote"** on MakerWorld (1,195 downloads, 277 prints, P2S-listed PLA profile) puts an ESP32 on a printed 3-inch brushless quad, but it flies from its own ESP32 handset, so phone control would mean rewriting its firmware ([MakerWorld](https://makerworld.com/en/models/1211264-diy-drone-frame-remote)).

### Your phone's operating system decides which apps you can use

Each option above relies on a different app, so the phone you own shapes the shortlist as much as the drone does.

Android covers everything in the table. An iPhone owner is limited to the 2020 ESP-Drone app, which CircuitDigest says is being phased out "due to compatibility issues with modern devices", to LiteWing's iOS listing, whose availability is unresolved, to the Crazyflie, and to browser-controlled builds ([CircuitDigest flight guide](https://circuitdigest.com/articles/start-flying-with-litewing)). No iOS app for flying Flix was found. Because ESP-FLY runs a modified ESP-Drone/CircuitDigest firmware, the maintained LiteWing app should in principle fly it, but nobody has documented that pairing. The cheapest way to reduce this risk is a spare Android phone used only as the drone's controller.

### Brownouts, vibration and Wi-Fi drops cause most failed flights

Builders' issue trackers show the same few failure modes, and **power sag comes first**: when the motors spin up, battery voltage dips and the electronics can reset. One ESP32-C3 build reset above about 20% throttle (≈700 mA) even with an added 10 µF capacitor, an MPU6050's data bus locked up at about 3.7 V and rebooted another drone, a LiteWing reboot-looped on a larger battery, and a LiteWing user found the v2.6 board's 3.3 V regulator too weak at motor start and suggested one rated for at least 800 mA ([esp-drone #85](https://github.com/espressif/esp-drone/issues/85); [esp-drone #105](https://github.com/espressif/esp-drone/issues/105); [LiteWing #7](https://github.com/jobitjoseph/LiteWing/issues/7); [LiteWing wiki comments](https://circuitdigest.com/litewing)). The cures are a battery that can supply the current (Flix's 15 A rule), never spinning motors on USB power, and an optional boost converter ([Flix troubleshooting](https://github.com/okalachev/flix/blob/master/docs/troubleshooting.md)).

Vibration from loose motors corrupts the drone's estimate of its own orientation. And without optical flow, every one of these drones drifts. LiteWing's guide says minor drift "is expected", and one user found the app's 0.6° trim step too coarse to correct it ([Flix assembly](https://github.com/okalachev/flix/blob/master/docs/assembly.md); [LiteWing flight guide](https://circuitdigest.com/articles/start-flying-with-litewing); [LiteWing #12](https://github.com/jobitjoseph/LiteWing/issues/12)).

The link fails too. Android phones drop the drone while mobile data is on, so fly in airplane mode with Wi-Fi enabled and VPNs off ([LiteWing flight guide](https://circuitdigest.com/articles/start-flying-with-litewing)). Drones fitted with LiteWing's positioning module logged frequent packet loss ([LiteWing #18](https://github.com/jobitjoseph/LiteWing/issues/18)). Flix warns that joining the drone through your home router adds delay compared with connecting to the drone's own access point ([Flix usage docs](https://github.com/okalachev/flix/blob/master/docs/usage.md)).

Software and parts cause trouble as well. Users report the ESP-Drone app connecting without moving the motors ([esp-drone #123](https://github.com/espressif/esp-drone/issues/123)). ESP-Drone requires version 5.0 of Espressif's ESP-IDF toolkit, while Flix requires version 3.3.10 of the ESP32 Arduino core, so install exactly those versions ([ESP-Drone README](https://github.com/espressif/esp-drone); [Flix usage docs](https://github.com/okalachev/flix/blob/master/docs/usage.md)). CircuitDigest also warns that some counterfeit MPU6050 chips fail at the 400 kHz bus speed ([CircuitDigest](https://circuitdigest.com/microcontroller-projects/DIY-wifi-controlled-drone)).

## Each feature beyond self-levelling adds sensors, grams and dollars

The features you listed form a ladder, and each rung adds a sensor, weight and a firmware requirement. The decisive break comes at GPS. Altitude hold and indoor position hold fit on a phone-flown ESP32 micro, but no phone-flown ESP32 firmware supports GPS navigation. ESP-FC, the most advanced ESP32 flight firmware, still lists both altitude hold and GPS navigation as to-do items ([ESP-FC README](https://github.com/rtlopez/esp-fc)). GPS therefore means a brushless build running ArduPilot, INAV or PX4, the established open-source autopilot firmwares.

### Altitude and indoor position hold fit on an ESP32 micro, with caveats

Height hold matters a great deal to a phone pilot, because it removes the need to adjust throttle continuously on a touchscreen. There are two ways to sense height. A barometer reads air pressure; even $24 toy drones like the E88 use one for altitude hold, so the technique is mature ([Circuit Digest E88 teardown](https://circuitdigest.com/review/everything-you-need-to-know-about-the-e88-drone-teardown)). A time-of-flight sensor measures distance to the floor and suits indoor flying. LiteWing's app offers a height-hold mode backed by its distance-sensor module, ESP-Drone implements both holds with its extension boards, and Flix leaves them to its community for now ([CircuitDigest](https://circuitdigest.com/articles/litewing-esp32-drone-gets-new-mobile-app); [ESP-Drone README](https://github.com/espressif/esp-drone); [Flix user builds](https://github.com/okalachev/flix/blob/master/docs/user.md)).

Optical flow, which works like an upside-down optical mouse, is less forgiving. It needs light and floor texture: Matek's 3901-L0X module, for example, specifies more than 60 lux and a working height of 8 cm to 2 m ([RDQ](https://www.racedayquads.com/products/matek-3901-l0x-optical-flow-lidar-sensor)). The software support is still rough. An open ESP-Drone issue reports that its position estimator stops working once an optical-flow sensor is added, and LiteWing's packet-loss reports come from drones fitted with its positioning module ([esp-drone #102](https://github.com/espressif/esp-drone/issues/102); [LiteWing #18](https://github.com/jobitjoseph/LiteWing/issues/18)).

Weight also limits which drones can take these sensors. ESP-FLY's 3 g payload leaves no room, while LiteWing's roughly 25 g payload does. In practice, LiteWing with its positioning module is today's only ready-made phone-flown ESP32 drone with both holds. A DIY ESP-Drone build on a printed 8520 frame can in principle match it for about $33 of sensors, but nobody has documented doing so yet.

### GPS forces a jump to a printed 5-inch ArduPilot quad and Android

GPS receivers with compasses weigh 14–32 g, several times ESP-FLY's entire 3 g payload, and no ESP32 phone firmware could use one anyway. The only ESP32-class printed design reported with GPS is Max Imagination's 2026 ESP-Blast: a 136 g quad with a PETG frame, barometer and GPS, costing about $155. Neither its files nor its control method have been confirmed ([Notebookcheck](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Tiny-open-source-3D-printed-drone-hits-67-mph-and-weighs-136-grams.1249623.0.html)). No well-documented printed folding "DJI-style" GPS camera drone exists. The printed folding designs found are FPV-only or have no build evidence ([MakerWorld 18650 Micro Foldable](https://makerworld.com/en/models/1917900-18650-micro-foldable-fpv-drone)).

**The phone app.** QGroundControl for Android is the only mature, actively maintained, full-featured phone ground station. It works with ArduPilot and PX4 (version 5.1.5, released 30 September 2026) and connects over USB-OTG cable, Bluetooth and Wi-Fi ([QGC tags](https://github.com/mavlink/qgroundcontrol/tags)). Its flight screen offers arm and takeoff, land, return-to-launch, pause, change altitude, tap-the-map "Go to location", orbit, region of interest, follow-me and mission start ([QGC Fly View](https://docs.qgroundcontrol.com/master/en/qgc-user-guide/fly_view/fly_view.html)). With ArduPilot, follow-me fails with "Home position not set" until the GPS has fixed a home point ([QGC source](https://github.com/mavlink/qgroundcontrol/blob/v5.1.5/src/FirmwarePlugin/APM/APMFirmwarePlugin.cc)).

iPhone owners have to choose among smaller apps whose maintenance status is unclear: SidePilot and MAV Pilot, both listed by ArduPilot; MicoAir's MicoPilot, in TestFlight beta; and CoreWing, which supports INAV and ArduPilot ([ArduPilot GCS list](https://ardupilot.org/copter/docs/common-choosing-a-ground-station.html); [MicoAir](https://micoair.com/?p=5823); [CoreWing](https://apps.apple.com/sr/app/corewing/id6755523356)). The SpeedyBee app configures Betaflight and INAV but not ArduPilot ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/speedybee-app/id1150315028)).

**The firmware.** ArduPilot is the safer choice for a phone-centred build. INAV's own documentation calls its support for MAVLink (the telemetry protocol QGroundControl speaks) "selective". It rejects the MAVLink takeoff command, and boards with 512 KB of flash memory, including every STM32F411 and STM32F722 board, have no MAVLink at all ([INAV Mavlink.md](https://github.com/iNavFlight/inav/blob/10.0.0-rc2/docs/Mavlink.md)). The F411-based IMLABS design above therefore cannot talk to QGroundControl.

**Flight controller and phone link.** Choose these together. H743 boards with built-in Bluetooth, such as the MicoAir743 V2 ($72.99, ArduPilot pre-installed) and the Holybro Kakute H7 V2 ($52, ESP32-C3 Bluetooth), connect to the phone without extra wiring, whereas the Bluetooth on SpeedyBee F405 V4 boards is "not currently usable by ArduPilot" ([Pyrodrone](https://pyrodrone.com/collections/micoair-products/products/micoair-h743-v2-2-6s-flight-controller-with-bluetooth-and-pre-loaded-with-ardupilot-30-30mm); [Holybro](https://holybro.com/products/kakute-h7-v2); [ArduPilot README](https://github.com/ArduPilot/ardupilot/blob/Copter-4.7.1/libraries/AP_HAL_ChibiOS/hwdef/speedybeef4v4/README.md)). For more range, a DroneBridge ESP32 board links the flight controller to the phone's Wi-Fi for 150 m or more (about 1 km over ESP-NOW if you add a second ESP32 on the ground), and a SiK telemetry radio reaches more than 300 m out of the box and plugs into an Android phone with a USB-OTG cable ([ArduPilot DroneBridge](https://ardupilot.org/copter/docs/common-esp32-telemetry.html); [Holybro SiK](https://holybro.com/products/sik-telemetry-radio-v3)). ExpressLRS (ELRS) 3.5 and later can carry radio control and telemetry on one link and forward the telemetry to the phone over the radio's Backpack Wi-Fi, provided the ELRS hardware is ESP-based and you accept a forced 1:2 telemetry ratio ([ELRS MAVLink](https://www.expresslrs.org/software/mavlink/)). If phone telemetry runs over 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi, use 868/915 MHz radio control to avoid the 2.4 GHz interference one mapping-drone builder reported ([Evan Briggs](https://evanbriggs358.github.io/)).

**Flying from the phone alone** is possible but discouraged. ArduPilot documents a ground-station-only setup: you disable the throttle failsafe, skip the radio arming check, set `RC_PROTOCOLS = 0`, and send a takeoff or mission-start command within seconds of arming, before the copter disarms itself ([ArduPilot GCS-only](https://ardupilot.org/copter/docs/common-gcs-only-operation.html)). Yet ArduPilot's joystick page says that "even if flying with a joystick, you should keep a regular transmitter/receiver connected and ready for use as a backup". QGroundControl warns that its on-screen thumbsticks are "not as responsive as using an RC Transmitter" ([ArduPilot Joystick](https://ardupilot.org/copter/docs/common-joystick.html); [QGC virtual joystick](https://docs.qgroundcontrol.com/master/en/qgc-user-guide/settings_view/virtual_joystick.html)). If the phone link drops, the ground-station failsafe triggers after 5 s by default and runs a return-to-launch (RTL), SmartRTL or landing. The copter stays in that mode even after the phone reconnects ([ArduPilot GCS failsafe](https://ardupilot.org/copter/docs/gcs-failsafe.html)).

The sound pattern is to command guided and autonomous flight from the phone while keeping an ELRS transmitter in hand, to set both the phone-link failsafe and the radio failsafe to return-to-launch or land, and to assign a transmitter switch to ArduPilot's "RC override enable" function so you can cut phone input instantly ([ArduPilot GCS-only](https://ardupilot.org/copter/docs/common-gcs-only-operation.html)).

### Video reaches the phone, but with lag and an Android bias

On the micro drones, the phone's Wi-Fi is already busy carrying control commands, and the cameras that fit are weak. ESP-FLY's maintainers say an ESP32 camera is "not directly" possible with the current firmware and support a 5.8 GHz analog all-in-one camera instead, which raises the drone from 25 to 28 g ([Seeed ESP-FLY repo](https://github.com/Seeed-Projects/Co-Create_ESP-FLY)). CircuitDigest's LiteWing tutorial adds a Wi-Fi camera module taken from a toy drone, running on its own Wi-Fi network and viewed in ordinary IP-camera apps, and notes video jitter when the motors start that a battery with a higher C rating fixes ([CircuitDigest tutorial](https://circuitdigest.com/tutorial/adding-wi-fi-camera-to-litewing-esp32-drone)). A Flix community build streams video from an ESP32-S3-CAM board ([Flix-Camera-Streaming](https://github.com/CatRey/Flix-Camera-Streaming)).

Simple ESP32-CAM streams manage about 10–15 frames per second from the common OV2640 camera, and the usual advice is to put control and video on separate Wi-Fi channels ([ElectronicsForU](https://www.electronicsforu.com/electronics-projects/low-cost-drone-powered-by-esp32-cam)). The best open ESP32 video system, hx-esp32-cam-fpv, reaches 640×360 at 30 fps up to 1024×576, with 90–110 ms latency. It warns, though, that image quality is "comparable to smartphone cameras from around 2005" ([hx-esp32-cam-fpv](https://github.com/RomanLut/hx-esp32-cam-fpv)).

For brushless quads, three routes reach a phone. The first is analog: plug-in 5.8 GHz receivers for Android's USB port cost $13–45 and deliver 640×480 at about 100 ms latency, which one reviewer reserves for "slow ground models, spectators and bench tests" ([Unmanned Tech review](https://www.unmannedtechshop.co.uk/blogs/knowledge-base/eachine-rotg02-android-fpv-receiver-setup-review); [brushlesswhoop](https://brushlesswhoop.com/usb-otg-fpv-receivers/)). iPhones cannot use them, because iOS supports external USB video devices only on USB-C iPads ([Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/776912)).

The second is OpenIPC digital video, which feeds the Android app PixelPilot through a USB Wi-Fi adapter (RTL8812AU). Alternatively, in APFPV mode the drone's video transmitter becomes a Wi-Fi access point the phone joins directly, viewable in PixelPilot or a browser, at 35–70 ms latency and 50–200 m range on the phone's own Wi-Fi ([PixelPilot](https://github.com/OpenIPC/PixelPilot); [OpenIPC APFPV](https://docs.openipc.org/use-cases/fpv/apfpv/apfpv/)). A September 2026 bench test measured OpenIPC at 42–58 ms at 1080p 60 fps, against 14–22 ms for analog and 28–38 ms for DJI O3 ([idostudio](https://idostudio.ai/insight/2026-09-22-testing-openipc-against-walksnail-and-dji-on-the-flight-bench)), and MakerWorld already hosts a printable 2-inch frame built around RunCam's WiFiLink2 OpenIPC camera for a quad of about 73 g ([MakerWorld](https://makerworld.com/en/models/1341984-wifilink2-openipc-2-inch-drone-frame-and-canopy)). The third is DJI, whose O4 air unit reaches a phone only when DJI Goggles 3 share the feed to the DJI Fly app ([DJI Goggles 3 FAQ](https://www.dji.com/goggles-3/faq)); Walksnail and HDZero video reach Android only through an HDMI input, which adds delay ([Oscar Liang](https://oscarliang.com/radiomaster-ax12/)).

### Phone transmitters for Betaflight remain bench-grade experiments

No maintained app flies a Betaflight or INAV quad from a phone. The documented routes are hobby projects: an ESP32 web joystick that feeds commands into the flight controller with about 50 m of range, a phone-browser ESP-NOW transmitter for ESP-FC that its author restricts to "ground testing" and props-off debugging, and a thesis project that turns app commands into Betaflight receiver signals ([Schwarz](https://michalschwarz.github.io/rc-controller/esp32/quadcopter/f450/asgard32/schema/2019/05/07/esp32-drone-v1.0.0.html); [Ishu1519](https://github.com/Ishu1519/ESP-FC-ESP-NOW-Web-RC); [MustafaQassmieh01](https://github.com/MustafaQassmieh01/ESP32-Communication-to-crsf)).

Betaflight's own guide labels a companion device acting as the sole receiver "bench-only". It recommends an override mode instead, in which a real radio receiver keeps the arm switch and the phone only overrides the sticks. Its default failsafe drops the quad after a 1.5 s grace period ([Betaflight MSP guide](https://betaflight.com/docs/wiki/guides/current/MSP-Companion-Computer-Control); [Betaflight Failsafe](https://betaflight.com/docs/wiki/guides/current/Failsafe)).

The phone features in ExpressLRS (its Wi-Fi web page and Backpack) and SpeedyBee's Bluetooth are documented for configuration and telemetry, not flying ([ELRS Wi-Fi](https://www.expresslrs.org/software/updating/wifi-updating/); [SpeedyBee app](https://apps.apple.com/app/speedybee-app/id1150315028)). Betaflight 2026.6 now runs experimentally on ESP32-S3 chips, but its new "Betaflight Bridge" is a configuration link for iPhones, not a control link ([Betaflight 2026.6 release notes](https://betaflight.com/docs/wiki/release/Betaflight-2026-6-Release-Notes)).

The workable compromise is a hybrid. A cheap ELRS radio does the flying: BetaFPV's LiteRadio 3 costs $32.99 and RadioMaster's Pocket $59.99–71.50. The phone handles video, configuration (the SpeedyBee app over Bluetooth, the Betaflight App on Android over a USB-OTG cable, and the ELRS web page) and simulator practice ([BetaFPV](https://betafpv.com/products/literadio-3-radio-transmitter); [RadioMaster](https://radiomasterrc.com/products/pocket-radio-controller-m2); [Betaflight 2026.6 release notes](https://betaflight.com/docs/wiki/release/Betaflight-2026-6-Release-Notes)). FPV educator Oscar Liang notes that touchscreen flying "doesn't effectively translate to real-world flying skills", and recommends a real radio to build muscle memory ([Oscar Liang](https://oscarliang.com/fpv-simulator/)).

### Toy transplants, Tello shells, blimps and planes are side roads

**Toy-drone transplants.** The cheapest shortcut is to move a $15–40 toy drone's electronics into a printed body, and it has the weakest evidence. A January 2026 teardown of the popular E88 toy drone found a flight chip with its markings removed, a BMP180 barometer for altitude hold, a proprietary 2.4 GHz link to its handset and a phone used only to show video, and concluded there is "no access to the flight controller, no programmable features, and no open-source code" ([Circuit Digest teardown](https://circuitdigest.com/review/everything-you-need-to-know-about-the-e88-drone-teardown)).

Some E88/E99 listings do advertise "APP control", and the WiFi UAV app (more than 2.5 million downloads, updated August 2026) offers on-screen sticks ([mwm.ai](https://mwm.ai/apps/wifi-uav/1471622385)). But no 2024–2026 transplant guide exists. Because the toy's flight tuning is fixed and hidden, a printed shell has to copy the donor's motor spacing, weight and balance, and a forum discussion warns that re-tuning "may not be possible if tuning parameters are not exposed" ([IntoFPV](https://intofpv.com/archive/index.php/thread-17014.html)).

**Other shortcuts.** Each has a catch. **Tello**, which had the best phone apps and printable shells, was discontinued in 2024 and is now available secondhand only ([DroneDJ](https://dronedj.com/2024/01/04/dji-education-drone-shutdown-us/)). **Drona Aviation's Pluto X** encourages students to print their own frames and has an app updated in 2026, but sells mainly in India at ₹14,000–20,000 ([ElectronicsForU](https://electronicsforu.com/technology-trends/arduino-is-for-electronics-raspberry-pi-is-for-computing-we-aim-to-become-that-for-drones-with-pluto); [Drona](https://www.dronaaviation.com/plutox)). **Blimps** are the gentlest phone-flown printable aircraft—Blimpduino 2.0 flies 20–30 minutes on a 500 mAh battery with an ESP8266 and a phone app—but helium lifts only about 1.1 g per litre, so every 100 g of blimp needs roughly 100 L of balloon ([HackSpace review](https://magazine.raspberrypi.com/articles/take-to-the-skies-blimpduino-review); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lifting_gas)). **Printed planes** suit the P2S, but Planeprint's newer models require lightweight LW-PLA, which you do not have, and all are flown by radio; MakerWorld's GASB ONE plane prints in PETG on a P2S profile (503 g, 21.7 h) but is radio-flown too ([Planeprint](https://www.planeprint.com/print); [MakerWorld](https://makerworld.com/en/models/2202921-gasb-one-world-s-most-printed-rc-plane-x-bambu)). **General-purpose maker control apps** are unsuitable for multirotors: Dabble's joystick has only 15 steps (−7 to +7), and Blynk's library is "orientated around home automation where a second or two does not matter" ([Dabble](https://ai.thestempedia.com/docs/dabble-app/gamepad-module/); [Blynk](https://community.blynk.cc/t/esp-blynk-library-blocking/36816)).

## Your four filaments cover nearly every printed part

### TPU 85A needs a 0.6 mm hotend and hand feeding on the P2S

Two of your four filaments need special handling. Bambu lists TPU 85A as incompatible with every AMS (automatic filament feeder) except the AMS HT, and even that may only serve as a sealed drying box. TPU 85A is supported only on 0.6 and 0.8 mm nozzles, not the 0.4 mm nozzle your P2S ships with, so you need a **$20.99 0.6 mm hotend** before you can print it ([Bambu TPU 85A/90A](https://us.store.bambulab.com/products/tpu-85a-tpu-90a); [Shop3DUniverse](https://shop3duniverse.com/products/bambu-lab-hotend-for-h2-p2s)).

Loading TPU 85A means removing the top glass, detaching the toolhead's PTFE feed tube and setting the spool beside and above the printer, then heating the nozzle to 250 °C and feeding the filament straight into the extruder, pressing Load slowly so it does not wrap around the gears. Unloading is also manual, and printing runs at 225 °C on a 30–35 °C bed ([Bambu Wiki TPU guide](https://wiki.bambulab.com/en/filament-acc/filament/tpu-85a-90a-printing-guide-for-p-series)).

Your TPU 65D depends on who made it. If it is a third-party filament, Bambu's documents treat it as "generic TPU", fed from an external spool rather than the AMS. If it is actually Bambu's "TPU for AMS", that grade is 68D and does feed through the AMS ([Bambu TPU for AMS](https://us.store.bambulab.com/products/tpu-for-ams); [AMS HT](https://us.store.bambulab.com/products/ams-ht)). Typical 65D filaments print at 225–245 °C, at only about 20–40 mm/s, and must be dried first ([3DDevice KLEMA 65D](https://3ddevice.com.ua/en/product/tpu-65d-filament-klema-1-kg-1-75-mm-black/)).

Bambu says TPU must be dried: 16 h at 90 °C on the P2S bed under a cover, or 8 h at 70 °C in an oven. It recommends drying PLA for 12 h at 60–70 °C and PETG for 12 h at 75–85 °C ([Bambu Wiki drying](https://wiki.bambulab.com/en/filament-acc/filament/dry-filament)). Keep a separate hotend for TPU if you later print carbon-fibre filaments: leftover fibres in the nozzle make TPU harder to feed ([Bambu Wiki TPU guide](https://wiki.bambulab.com/en/filament-acc/filament/tpu-85a-90a-printing-guide-for-p-series)).

### Datasheets favour PLA for stiffness, PETG for heat and TPU for impact

Bambu's own datasheets overturn the common assumption that PETG is the stronger frame filament. PLA is about 40% stiffer, and holds 89% of its strength across layers against PETG's 68%. PETG's advantages are heat resistance and slightly more toughness. The two TPUs belong to a different class of material entirely.

PLA's weakness is heat. It starts to bend under load at 54 °C, below the 69–71 °C that a sun-baked car dashboard reaches within an hour ([ASU](https://news.asu.edu/20180516-discoveries-asu-study-hot-cars-can-hit-deadly-temperatures-within-one-hour)). Hard TPU has a different weakness: it slowly deforms under sustained pressure. After 24 h squeezed at 70 °C, BASF's 64D keeps 55% of the deformation permanently, so TPU should never be clamped under motor or electronics-stack bolts ([BASF 64D TDS](https://move.forward-am.com/hubfs/AES%20Documentation/Flexible%20Filaments/TPU%2064D/TDS/Ultrafuse_TPU_64D_TDS_EN_v1.1.pdf)).

Field reports on frame materials conflict. Grasshopper5's designer says PLA will not survive near hot motors and the video transmitter, and the Sub-250 author warns that PLA softens in the sun ([Robofusion](https://docs.robofusion.net/projects/grasshopper5-fpv-frame); [Printables](https://www.printables.com/model/942571-sub-250g-autonomous-drone-platform)). Yet the Teardrop Quad's author chose PLA-Pro for "stiffness and layer adhesion" and flew a survey mission on it, and one 5-inch designer concluded that "PETG is too brittle, Nylon is too flexible, PC and PLA work well" while noting that PLA's melting point is too low ([ArduPilot Discourse](https://discuss.ardupilot.org/t/free-stl-files-now-available-for-download-for-basic-quad-copter-all-3d-printed/131750); [MakerWorld G4ZO](https://makerworld.com/en/models/1336260)). The Crab frame's designer saw clear PETG crack on hard landings, PETG-CF survive crashes from up to 10 m, and PAHT-CF survive a fall of about 100 m onto grass ([MakerWorld Crab](https://makerworld.com/en/models/1284211-crab-4-5-inch-fully-3d-printed-fpv-drone-frame)). The reports conflict because each tests a different way of failing. Stiffness and layer adhesion favour PLA, heat favours PETG, and crash energy favours fibre-filled nylon or TPU. Oscar Liang's long-standing practice, "TPU and PETG 99% of the times, TPU for flexible parts and PETG for rigid parts", is the sensible default ([Oscar Liang](https://oscarliang.com/3d-printer/)).

### Orientation, infill and inserts matter more than filament brand

Print arms lying flat, so that bending loads run along the layers. Across layers, PETG keeps only 68% of its strength, and carbon-fibre filaments only 47% ([PA6-CF TDS](https://store.bblcdn.com/b2abc59ac250492b979a52f1ce3e61b3.pdf)). Use solid infill wherever loads pass through. A peer-reviewed sub-250 g PETG frame printed its arms at 0.2 mm layers and 100% infill and its base plate at 40%. It reached a safety factor of 1.35 in 5 m/s crash simulations ([Drones 2025, 9, 789](https://www.mdpi.com/2504-446X/9/11/789)). Flix also prints at 100%, while ESP-FLY's tiny frame uses only 10%.

**Screws and mounting.** Your soldering station doubles as an insert tool. Heat brass threaded inserts 10–20 °C above the print temperature (about 225 °C for PLA, 245 °C for PETG) and press them into straight holes about 1 mm deeper than the insert. Push each one about 90% of the way in, then press it flush with a flat tool and hold until the plastic sets ([CNC Kitchen](https://www.cnckitchen.com/blog/tipps-amp-tricks-fr-gewindeeinstze-im-3d-druck-3awey)). Inserts cost about €9–10 per hundred ([CNC Kitchen store](https://cnckitchen.store/products/heat-set-insert-m3-x-5-7-100-pieces)). Make the plastic wall around any threaded hole at least as thick as the screw diameter, choose motor screws about 2 mm longer than the arm is thick so they cannot reach the motor windings, and mount the flight controller and camera on soft TPU while bolting the motors tightly to a stiff arm ([Hubs](https://www.hubs.com/knowledge-base/how-assemble-3d-printed-parts-threaded-fasteners/); [Oscar Liang screws](https://oscarliang.com/check-motor-screws-touching-winding); [Oscar Liang soft mounting](https://oscarliang.com/soft-mounting-fc-motors/); [Flix assembly](https://github.com/okalachev/flix/blob/master/docs/assembly.md)).

**Stiffness** is the hidden cost of printed frames. Betaflight's tuning notes say "stiff frames… will accept more D", meaning a stiff frame can be tuned to fly more crisply. A PETG ManaFly is reportedly about 61% less stiff than the carbon frame it was compared with ([Betaflight 4.3 tuning notes](https://betaflight.com/docs/wiki/tuning/4-3-Tuning-Notes); [MakerWorld ManaFly](https://makerworld.com/en/models/2000546-beta-manafly-3-generative-fpv-drone-frame)). On the other hand, a ManaFly frame costs only about $1 in filament.

**If you outgrow your four filaments**, three additions stand out. **PET-CF** ($44.99) is the stiffest per gram in Bambu's range, keeps its shape under load up to 182 °C and barely absorbs moisture. **PA6-CF** (from $42.99) is the toughest carbon option but absorbs up to 2.35% water and must be kept dry. **ASA** ($29.99/kg) keeps its shape up to 92 °C and resists sunlight, which suits outdoor mounts and canopies ([Bambu PET-CF](https://us.store.bambulab.com/products/pet-cf); [PET-CF TDS](https://store.bblcdn.com/9690d6226f024acab2ba0dd52dabb654.pdf); [Bambu PA6-CF](https://us.store.bambulab.com/products/pa6-cf); [PA6-CF TDS](https://store.bblcdn.com/b2abc59ac250492b979a52f1ce3e61b3.pdf); [Bambu ASA](https://us.store.bambulab.com/products/asa-filament); [ASA TDS](https://store.bblcdn.com/ad7b08230c164e72856cffbe06bb7dc9.pdf); [Additive-X](https://www.additive-x.com/blog/3d-printing-for-drones-materials-parts-and-applications)).

### LiPo discipline: 4.20 V full, 3.85 V storage, never unattended

Oscar Liang's battery guide sets the numbers. A standard LiPo is full at **4.20 V per cell** (4.35 V for high-voltage LiHV) and should be stored at **3.80–3.85 V** if it will sit for more than about two weeks. Land at 3.5–3.6 V per cell and never drain below 3.0 V. Charge at 1C or less (about 0.45 A for a 450 mAh battery), always with the balance lead connected and never unattended, and retire any puffed (swollen) battery, because the damage is permanent. LiPo bags "slow fires but don't contain them", so if you store batteries in a metal ammo box, remove its rubber seal and drill vent holes ([Oscar Liang LiPo guide](https://oscarliang.com/lipo-battery-guide/)).

Never put lithium batteries in household trash or recycling. Tape the terminals and take them to a battery drop-off or hazardous-waste point ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/recycle/used-lithium-ion-batteries)). Spare batteries may travel only in carry-on luggage, each limited to 100 Wh, or 101–160 Wh for up to two spares with airline approval ([FAA PackSafe](https://www.faa.gov/hazmat/packsafe/lithium-batteries)). A 6S 1,300 mAh pack is about 29 Wh.

Test every new build on the bench in a fixed order: check for short circuits with the multimeter, power it up for the first time through a current limit, test each motor's spin direction with the props off, test the failsafe with the props off (ArduPilot's own failsafe instructions say to remove the propellers if a battery is connected), and only then fit the props, checking that each one matches its motor's direction ([ArduPilot radio failsafe](https://ardupilot.org/copter/docs/radio-failsafe.html)).

For phone-flown drones, the failsafe test must cover losing the app, not just the radio. Kill the app or the phone's Wi-Fi during a low hover over a soft surface, and confirm the drone cuts out or lands. Comet Drone, for example, stops its motors after 500 ms without Wi-Fi ([Comet-Drone](https://github.com/CoreCometIndustries/Comet-Drone)). On a 6S ArduPilot build, raise the low-battery failsafe voltage: the 10.5 V default (`BATT_LOW_VOLT`) equals about 3.5 V per cell only on a 3S battery, so on 6S it would never trigger before the pack was ruined ([ArduPilot battery failsafe](https://ardupilot.org/copter/docs/failsafe-battery.html)).

## A four-stage path runs from a $60 kit to GPS missions

The evidence points to a staged path. Each stage keeps the tools and skills of the one before, and you move up a weight class only after mastering the one below.

| Stage | Build | What it teaches | Approx. cost | Phone's role | Weight class |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Hover indoors | ESP-FLY kit; print spare frames and covers in PLA from the free MakerWorld remix | Soldering motors, loading firmware, trimming, battery care | ≈$117: kit, HexaCharger, two spare batteries, prop remover, cable | Full stick control (ESP-Drone app) | Under 100 g |
| 2. Build from modules and add holds | Flix on its printed frame (Android), or ESP-Drone on a printed 8520 frame with VL53L1X and PMW3901; ready-made alternative: LiteWing with its positioning module | Wiring, sensor calibration, altitude and position hold | ≈$30–60 plus ≈$33 of sensors; LiteWing $49 plus sensors | Full stick control, with height hold on LiteWing | Under 100 g |
| 3. Real stick skills and FPV (optional) | $33–72 ELRS radio and a simulator; ready-made Air65 II, or the SaTL frame printed in TPU 65D; Android plug-in receiver for video on the phone | Muscle memory, Betaflight setup through phone apps | ≈$170–215 | Video, configuration, simulator | Under 100 g |
| 4. GPS autonomy | Grasshopper5 in PETG (PET-CF later) with MicoAir H743 V2, M10 GPS and ELRS radio | Missions, tap-to-fly, compass calibration, failsafe tuning | ≈$507 (≈$685 with optical flow, Remote ID and HD video) | QGroundControl ground station | Over 250 g; registration rules apply |

Two choices shape the path. The first is your phone. If it is an iPhone, stage 1 still works through the iOS ESP-Drone app, though nobody has confirmed that app runs on current iOS. From stage 2 onward, nearly every tool (QGroundControl, Mavlink Joystick, plug-in video receivers, PixelPilot) is Android-only, so a spare Android phone becomes part of the kit. The second is whether you mainly want video. If so, skip the ESP32 camera experiments and go to stage 3, where a $12 analog camera and a $13–45 plug-in receiver give a usable picture. ESP-FLY's 3 g payload can also carry that analog camera for indoor viewing.

## Conclusion

"Flying a printed drone from my phone" turns out to describe two distinct hobbies with a gap between them. In one, a Wi-Fi micro drone is flown with the phone as its transmitter. In the other, a GPS quad flies missions with the phone as its planner. In between sit the whoop-to-5-inch FPV quads that dominate the printing sites, where the phone is only a screen. Choosing between the two hobbies matters more than any choice of frame or filament. It also changes what the printer is for. For micros, the printer makes the whole airframe in half an hour. For FPV and GPS builds it mostly makes cheap, repairable accessories and replacement frames, because a printed frame is less stiff than carbon and gains instead in repair cost and design freedom.

The ecosystem is also moving toward you. LiteWing's actively updated app is replacing ESP-Drone's 2020 one. Flix has its own circuit board and position control in development. Betaflight now runs experimentally on ESP32 chips, and INAV 10 is adding MAVLink missions ([INAV releases](https://github.com/iNavFlight/inav/releases)). Expect hold modes and better links to reach sub-100 g printable designs. The purchases that carry forward through every stage are an Android phone, ESP32-S3 boards, distance and optical-flow sensors, and an ELRS radio. Filament is the cheapest part of the project and the one with the least influence on how it turns out.
